The Acts of the Apostles Part I – General Information

Introduction

The Acts of the Apostles is a fascinating book that narrates many intriguing episodes, including things such as tongues of fire (2:3), miraculous healings (3:1-10), avenging angels (12:23), earthquakes (16:26), shipwrecks (27:41-44), harrowing prison escapes (5:17-21; 9:23-25) , riots (19:23-40), murderous plots (25:1-3), courtroom drama (23:1-10), exorcism (19:13-17)

snake bites (28:1-6), tragic accident (20:9), humour (12:13-16), and naked man running (19:16). However, more than just a collection of astonishing stories, the book of Acts is a brilliant theological masterpiece that recounts the origins and development of the Christian mission from Jerusalem to Rome. At every turn of events, giving witness to the gospel of and about Jesus Christ takes the center stage.

The Acts of the Apostles occupies a distinct place among the New Testament (NT) books. Unlike other books of the NT, it gives a historical overview of the earliest stage of Christianity. It follows through the story of Jesus movement and is a sequel to the Gospel according to Luke. In fact, Luke and Acts are considered as one book in two volumes. Below, we shall explore more on the relation between and narrative unity of the Gospel according to Luke and the Book of the Acts of the Apostles.

Luke–Acts Relationship

LUKE-ACTS is a traditional abbreviation for the Gospel according to Luke and the Acts of the Apostles. The title calls attention to the conviction that the two documents, separated in the canon by the Fourth Gospel, are two volumes of a single literary project.

The separation must have taken place early. We have no manuscripts in which they appear joined, and the patristic writers, who know of their common authorship treated them separately. Reasons for their separation may have been straightforward.

  • The first volume fits well among the other Gospels, but Acts looks unlike any of the other NT writings.
  • Acts provide a fine introduction to the letters of Paul. We notice that Acts ends with Paul in Rome, and the Pauline collection ordinarily began with his Letter to the Romans.
  • Acts presents a portrait of Paul that emphasizes his place within the larger mission of the church.

Thus, Luke and Acts from early on, performed different functions, thereby causing a separation of the two according to their respective foci: Luke as Gospel and Acts as introduction

to the Pauline and other apostolic literature. In other words, although Acts is now separated from the Gospel of Luke by the Fourth Gospel that is placed between them, its present canonical order allows Acts to function as a bridge between the story of Jesus as recounted in the four Gospels and the story of the church as depicted in Acts and it provides a perfect framework for reading the apostolic epistles of Paul, James, Peter, John and Jude which follow it in the canon.

Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts

The narrative unity refers to the fact that Luke has intended one, continuous, unified narrative form for Luke-Acts. There is evidence to support the claim that Luke and Acts are written by the same author and the two books are intrinsically united. For instance, the prefaces of the Gospel according to Luke (1:1-4) and of the Acts of the Apostles (1:1-2) point to the literary unity of the two works. The author addresses the same reader, namely, Theophilus. The author also refers to his Gospel as “the first book” (Acts 1:1) indicating that he is the author of both volumes. The ending of the Gospel leads smoothly into the beginning of Acts, resuming various theological themes and events:

  • Proof of Christ’s resurrection (Luke 24:36-43; Acts 1:3)
  • Instruction to wait in Jerusalem for the gift of the Spirit (Luke 24:48; Acts 1:4-5)
  • The commission of the apostles to be witnesses of the resurrection (Luke 24:48; Acts 1:8)
  • Two accounts of Christ’s ascension (Luke 24:50-53; Acts 1:9)

Many scholars have noted that Luke appears to have outlined, as shown above, the two narratives in ways that are remarkably similar. In the Gospel the ministry of Jesus begins when the Holy Spirit comes upon him; then Jesus preaches a sermon claiming that a text from Isaiah explains why this has happened. In Acts, the mission of the church begins when the Holy Spirit descends upon the believers at Pentecost; then Peter preaches a sermon claiming that a text from Joel explains why this has happened. Through these similarities perhaps the author wanted to tell the stories in an artistic way, or possibly he hoped that the similarities would help people to remember key moments in the histories of Jesus and the church. Theologically, he may have wanted to present the church’s life and mission as a reduplication of the life and work of Jesus.

Besides these, there is also parallelism in the motif of ‘Journeys’. Jesus’ journey to Jerusalem (Luke 9:51– 19:44) is devoted to the teaching of Jesus. Similarly, in Acts, journeys of apostles and Paul are devoted to the teaching of disciples and apostles.

The structure of Luke-Acts follows a geographical movement beginning in Jerusalem and extending to the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8). Since Jerusalem and its temple have special geographical and theological significance, Jesus’ ministry and mission is naturally centripetal or a movement towards its center (Jerusalem), while in Acts, the disciples’ activities are centrifugal or movement away from its center (Jerusalem) to the ends of the earth. Both the structure and content of Luke-Acts emphasize the universal mission of salvation that extends to the farthest corners of the world.

Luke and Acts share many common themes, vocabulary, and ideas that help shape a theological and thematic unity. Following are some examples: (1) Both narratives begin in Jerusalem (Luke 1:5-25; Acts 1:1-8); (2) The departure of Jesus both in Luke 24:50-53 and Acts 1:6-11; (3) Jesus’ command that the disciples “stay in the city until endowed with power from on high” (Luke 24:49; Acts 1,4) and they preach ‘repentance and forgiveness of sins (Luke 24:47; Acts 2:38; 3:19; 5:31); (4) Action of the Spirit in Luke 3:21-22 and in Acts 2:1-13; (5) Theme of fulfilment in Luke 4:16-30 and in Acts 2:14-40 and (6) His reference to “witnesses” (Luke 24:48; Acts 1:8,22; 2:32; 3:15; 4:33).

Thus, Luke tells both the story of Jesus in the Gospel and that of the growing, developing, nascent, Christian Church in his second volume. While the Gospel speaks about God’s saving activity in and through the historical Jesus beginning from the birth of John the Baptist and ending with Ascension, the Acts depicts the story of the propagation of faith in Christ and the growth of the Church beginning from the Early Christian Community in Jerusalem till Paul’s missionary activity in Rome. Basically, the book of Acts describes the continuation of the work of the Risen Lord by human beings who served as witnesses to him.

Authorship

The narrative unity of Luke-Acts implies a common authorship of the Gospel and Acts. Although the author’s name is not identified anywhere in the text, he has traditionally been identified with the Luke who is mentioned in several epistles (Phlm 24; Col 4: 14; 2 Tim 4:11) as a companion, a fellow worker of Paul and was also a physician who was faithful to Paul in a final imprisonment.

There are arguments both for and against if Luke should be accepted as a historical author of Luke-Acts. Below we shall discuss some of these arguments.

Arguments for considering Luke as NOT the author

Those who hold this view, argue that the author of Acts could not have been Luke, the disciple of Paul because if he were a real companion, there would have been evidences of better familiarity with Paul and his views. Many of the views expressed by the Paul of Acts stand in clear contrast to the views finding expression in Paul’s genuine letters.

-          Paul’s attitude to the Law in Acts 16:1-5; 21:15-20 is positive in contrast to what is found in his letters;

-          The Paul of Acts 17:22-34 excuses the ignorance of God in contrast to what we find in Rom 1;

-          The author of Acts considers an apostle to be one of the Twelve, who had been a witness to the earthly ministry of Jesus starting from his baptism, while Paul’s consistent argument is that he is one of the apostles.

-          There are certain historical discrepancies in the details provided by Acts on the one hand and the letters of Paul on the other. For example, Acts account does not mention anything about Paul’s visit to Arabia subsequent to his conversion as related by Paul himself in Gal 1:17. Similarly, Paul does not mention anything about Ananias in the events immediately following his conversion unlike Acts 9:10-17.

Arguments for considering Luke as the author

Despite the above objections, the general view among scholars is that the author of the we- sections is indeed the author of the whole book. The we-sections are those passages in the book where we see that there is sudden shift from the usual third person narrative to the first-person plural. These passages are Acts 16:10-17; 20:5-21:18; and 27:1-28:16. The obvious implication is that the author was with Paul on the occasions when these episodes took place.

Some of the reasons for considering Luke as the author of Luke-Acts are:

-          Acts 1:1 mentions that an earlier work was written to the same person in which the words and deeds of Jesus were narrated. This undoubtedly is a good description of the gospel of Luke.

-          Both Luke 1:3 and Acts 1:1 mentions dedication to a certain Theophilus.

-          The kind of literary preface found in the beginning of both Luke and Acts is common in classical Greek literature, but not found in any of the other NT books.

-          The end of Luke fits in well with the beginning of Acts.

-          Only in Lk 9:28 and Acts 1:13 we find the name of John before that of James in the lists of apostles.

-          Only Luke and Acts refer to Jesus’ post-resurrection appearances in Judea as against the Galilean appearances spoken of by Matthew and Mark.

-          Some of the salient features such as interest in Gentiles, sympathetic treatment of women, frequent mention of the Holy Spirit, etc., are common to both writings.

-          Style and language of the two are closely similar.

Luke – the Person

Luke is believed to be a Syrian of Antioch, by profession a physician, the disciple of the apostles, and later a follower of Paul. Of the four evangelists, he had the best control of Greek. It is not clear that he knew either Hebrew or Aramaic; but he certainly knew the LXX, as seen not only in his citations of Scripture but also in his heavy use of Septuagintal style in appropriate parts of his work. This ability in Greek has caused many to posit that the evangelist was a Gentile convert to Christianity. Luke does not claim to be an eyewitness to the ministry of Jesus (Luke 1:1– 4); he was likely a second-generation Christian convert or possibly a God-fearer, whose native tongue was Greek.

Date of Composition

There are diverse opinions regarding the date of composition of the book of Acts. Some scholars think that the book must have been written in the early 60s because Acts ends its account of church history in the early 60s. To consolidate their argument, they claim the following:

a.       Acts does not talk about the persecutions that came under Nero (which took place around 64 CE).

b.        It does not even mention the martyrdoms of its principal characters (James the brother of Jesus, Peter, and Paul).

c.       It does not say anything about the destruction of Jerusalem (70 CE) or describe what happened to the community of Christians there.

d.      Luke shows no awareness of any of Paul’s letters, not even of Galatians or 2 Corinthians, with which Acts differs in some details; this suggests that Acts was written well before the Pauline corpus was assembled or the letters were widely circulated.

However other scholars argue that if Acts was written in early 60s, then Luke’s Gospel might have to be dated earlier than most people think, and if Luke used the Gospel of Mark as a source for his Gospel, then that book would have to be dated earlier still. None of this is impossible, and a few scholars do subscribe to a system of “early dating” that places the whole Mark – Luke – Acts complex within the lifetime of Paul (i.e., before 62 – 67, depending on which date for Paul’s death one adopts).

Most scholars think that the book of Acts was written shortly after the Gospel of Luke and that it can be dated along with that Gospel to mid-80s or between 70 and 90 CE. They argue that Luke’s depended on Mark, which is almost certain to have been written around the year 65. Moreover, there are clear indications in Luke that it was written after the destruction of the Temple took place in 70 CE. Passages such as Luke 19:43-44 and 21:20-24 point to the fact that the author was aware of the events of 70 CE. So, the general position among the scholars is that Acts was written between 70 and 90 CE.

The place of writing must be Rome because Luke-Acts ends with an incident in Rome.

Sources of Luke’s Information

It is evident from the work itself that Luke was only an eyewitness of a small portion of the transactions which he relates. He joined the apostle on his second missionary journey, and then appears to have been separated from him for several years, and afterwards accompanied him on his third missionary journey to Jerusalem; consequently, he must have received at second hand the whole of the first portion of the Acts, and much of the second. So, the question arises as to from whose testimony, or from what documents, was his history composed?

The sources of Luke’s information may be considered threefold: Personal observation, oral testimony, and perhaps written documents.

(i)     Personal Observation: the first and most direct source of information was personal observation. Luke was the companion of Paul during a part of his travels and was an eyewitness of several of the transactions which he relates. He was with Paul during his first visit to Philippi, he accompanied him on his last journey to Jerusalem, and sailed with him to Rome. In all probability he was present at Jerusalem when the apostle was arrested; and it is also likely that he was at Caesarea during a portion of the two years of the apostle’s imprisonment in that city, for it was from Caesarea that he embarked with him to Rome. The account of the voyage to Rome bears indubitable marks of being written by one who was present. Thus, then, the information contained in Ch. 16:8-40; 20, 21, 27, and 28, and perhaps also in Ch. 22–26, was derived from direct observation.

(ii)   Oral Testimony: The second source of information is that derived from the testimony of others. Now here Luke would be chiefly indebted to Paul himself. From his own mouth he would learn the account of that apostle’s conversion and missionary journeys, so that the information contained in the whole of the second part of the Acts (Ch. 13–28) may be accounted for without having recourse to the supposition of written sources. When in Judea, where he probably resided during the two years of Paul’s imprisonment at Caesarea, he would procure information from James and the church of Jerusalem: from them he might derive his accounts of the ascension, the miracle of Pentecost, the acts of Peter, the dispute between the Hellenists and the Hebrews, the martyrdom of Stephen and of the Apostle James. And there is also the information which he would obtain from the church of Caesarea: in that city he met with Philip the evangelist (Acts 21:8), and perhaps also with Cornelius the devout centurion. From this source he would derive his information concerning the evangelistic labours in Samaria, the conversion of the Ethiopian eunuch, the visions made to Peter and Cornelius, and the particulars connected with the death of Herod Agrippa I. Thus, by far the greater portion, if not the whole of the first part of the Acts, would be obtained from those who were directly connected with the transactions recorded, “who were eyewitnesses and ministers of the word.”

(iii)   Written Documents: Most biblical critics interpret the introduction of Luke’s Gospel as a declaration on the part of the author that he availed himself of written documents in preparing that work. Therefore, probably he also employed them in the composition of his history of the apostles. Besides, it is a remarkable fact that although the style of the Acts is substantially the same throughout, yet there is a greater number of Hebraisms in the first part, where he depended on information at second hand, than in the second part, where he was indebted to his own observation.

Purpose of the Book

The main purpose of Luke-Acts is to provide credible information about the beginning of Christianity (Lk 1:1-4). Such a purpose presupposes that in the society represented by Theophilus there was a great interest in the new religious movement and that there were already quite a few accounts of Jesus in circulation. And now the purpose is to write a reliable account to people like Theophilus. It is often presumed that the purpose of Acts is basically historical, and it is imagined to be the first history of the Church. However, one cannot speak of one exclusive purpose for such a book. The author may have been influenced by several factors.

Other popular suggestions concerning the purpose of the book are:

a.       He wanted to offer an irenic portrayal of Christian origins that would help to unify a church that was becoming increasingly diverse.

b.      He wanted to demonstrate the political innocence of Christians in a way that would forestall further persecution from the Roman government (see 18:13-15; 23:29; 25:8,18-19,25; 26:31-32; cf. Luke 23:4,14-15,20,22).

c.       He wanted to convince pagans to adopt the new faith and/or challenge them to adopt the values and commitment to justice that this faith promoted.

d.      He wanted to explain how Christianity had become a largely gentile religion in a way that would allow Christians to be viewed as the legitimate heirs of God’s promises to Israel.

e.       He wanted to present a long-term theology of mission for a church that had come to recognize that the second coming of Christ might lie in the distant future.

All of these points may be subsumed under a basic and obvious goal: Luke write the book of Acts because he wanted to tell of the story of the church’s early years in a way that would both inspire and challenge his readers.

Addressee of the Book

Theophilus is explicitly named as the recipient (Luke 1:3; Acts 1:1), but Luke probably intended both volumes to be read by more than just his patron, who had provided financial support for the investigation and writing and to whom the works had been dedicated. Some

scholars suggest that Theophilus, whose name means “lover of God,” could be a fictitious character invented to address everyone who loves God. While this theory is appealing, the suggestion remains purely speculative. Our knowledge of Luke’s real audience is limited, and any conclusion is therefore tentative. Luke-Acts appear to have been written for a mixed (predominantly Gentile Christian) community with large social distinction (e.g., between the rich and the poor). They were also made insecure and uncertain through the distorted teachings of false teachers (Acts 20:29-30). Probably they were losing their faith in the second coming of the Lord. Luke is writing these books to strengthen the faith of his readers by highlighting the true teaching, the continuity with the beginnings and especially the reliability of the Gospel message.

Literary Forms

Five notable literary forms are found in Acts: (a) Narratives; (b) Summaries; (c) Journeys; (d) Parallelisms; and (e) Speeches.

a.      Narratives: Much of Acts is told in dramatic story form, mainly recounting the progress and geographical expansion of the word of God beginning in Jerusalem and ending in Rome, then epitomized as “the ends of the earth” (1:8). Luke’s narration of the witness and mission of the disciples includes visions and miraculous episodes similar to the stories found in his “first account” (1:1). Since the preface of Luke’s Gospel could function as the main preface to his two- volume work, Acts seems to move beyond the narrative framework of an ancient biography, which is the form of the Gospel, into that of a properly historical account.

b.      Summaries: There are four summary passages that describe the ideal life of the early church (Acts 2:42-47; 4:32-35; 5:12-16; 8:1b-4). These passages only appear in the earlier part of Acts. Besides these four major summaries, there are seven other minor summary statements that function like progress reports of the spread of the word of God (2:47b; 6:7; 9:31; 12:24; 16:5; 19:20; 28:30-31). Except for the last summary statement (28:30-31), six of them serve as transition statements to help link together the episodes and move along the plot of Acts. It is claimed that Luke’s usage of summaries is “typical of ancient historiographical works”.

c.       Journeys: Another literary device is journeys or travel narrative. The Gospel of Luke contains one long travel narrative (9:51 – 19:27) in which Jesus teaches the meaning and cost of discipleship as he and his disciples travel from Galilee to Jerusalem. Acts contains three missionary journeys of Paul (13:4–14:26; 15:36–18:22; 18:23–21:17) and one sea voyage from Caesarea to Italy (27:1–28:16). The four “We Passages” (16:10-17; 20:5-15; 21:1-8; 27:1–28:16) are located within these travel narratives. As a literary device, the author employs the pronoun “we” to express his personal involvement and presence during Paul’s missionary journeys. The term “the Way” (Acts 9:2; 19:9,23; 22:4; 24:22), which is used in Acts five times as a designation for the Christian movement, is another way that Luke uses the journey motif.

d.      Parallelism: Scholars have long recognized many parallels between the two volumes. The most notable correspondences are the parallel trials of Jesus (Luke 22:54 – 23:47) and Paul (Acts 21:30 – 27:43) whereby each of them is declared innocent. Luke seems to have structured Acts according to the parallel witness and mission of Peter (Chapters 1–12) and Paul (Chapters 13–28), including the following similarities: healing a person who is crippled (3:1–26; 14:8–17); confronting magicians (8:9–24; 13:6–12) and raising the dead (9:36–43; 20:9–12).

e.       Speeches: There are twenty-eight speeches in Acts.

  • Eight by peter (1:16-22; 2:14b-36,38-39; 3:12b-26; 4:8b-12,19b-20; 5:29b-32; 10:34-43; 11:5-17; 15:7b-11)
  • Ten by Paul (13:16b-41; [and Barnabas] 14:15-17; 17:22-31; 20:18b-35; 22:1-21; 24:10b-21; 25:8-11; 26:2-23; 27:21-26; 28:17b-20)
  • One by Jesus (1:4-5,7-8)
  • Three by other disciples: Stephen (7:2-53); James (15:13b-21); and Twelve (6:2b-4)
  • Six by nonbelievers: Gamaliel (5:35b-39); Demetrius (19:35-40); the town clerk in Ephesus (19:35-40); Gallio (18:14b-15); Tertullus (24:2-8); Festus (25:14-27)

Almost a third of Acts is made up of speeches. They vary in type: missionary, evangelizing, indictment, defense, farewell, constitutive, political, and exhortation. Although they could function as “narrative filler”, they are an important “subform in ancient Greek historiography, used to produce a dramatic effect and often serving to aid the author’s purpose in writing”. The speeches in Acts are Lucan compositions, not verbatim reports. Like other ancient historians, Luke created and edited the speeches using his own language and style and allowing the speaker to say what was most appropriate for the occasion.

In summary, Luke has employed these five literary forms quite skilfully to achieve his theological and apologetic aim of persuading his readers that the universal mission is part of God’s great plan for both Jews and Gentiles.

Overview of the Book of Acts

The book of Acts opens with a preface to Theophilus (1:1-5), an account of Jesus’ ascension (1:6-11), and a brief narrative of how Matthias was chosen to replace Judas Iscariot as the twelfth apostle (1:12-26). Then, on the day of Pentecost, the holy spirit fills about 120 believers (1:15), who speak in tongues (2:1-13), and Peter preaches to the crowd, gaining many new converts (2:37-41). The marks of the early church are described (2:42-47). The healing of a lame man (3:1-10) leads to another sermon by Peter (3:11-26) and to the arrest of Peter and John by Jewish authorities (4:1-31). The Jerusalem church practices communal sharing of possessions, and two believers, Ananias and Sapphira, are struck dead by God for trying to take advantage of this arrangement (4:32–5:11). Luke then relates another arrest of the apostles and records that they were spared further suffering due to both miraculous intervention and the advice of a tolerant rabbi, Gamaliel (5:12-42). A dispute between Hellenists and Hebrews in the church

leads to the appointment of seven men to exercise leadership in the community (6:1-7); one of them, Stephen, is stoned to death by hostile Jews after preaching a sermon accusing them of unfaithfulness (6:8–8:1).

Luke relates how persecution against the church in Jerusalem led the church to expand geographically and to grow in ethnic diversity. Philip, one of the seven, brings the gospel to Samaria (where Peter has a confrontation with Simon Magus), and he also leads an Ethiopian eunuch to be baptized into the faith (8:2-40). A persecutor of the church, Saul (also known as Paul), has a vision of Jesus that transforms him into a passionate missionary for the faith (9:1- 31). Peter heals Aeneas in Lydda (9:32-35), raises Dorcas (Tabitha) from the dead (9:36-43), and baptizes a gentile centurion, Cornelius, after receiving a vision about what is clean and unclean (10:1–11:18). Barnabas and Saul become leaders of a gentile mission at Antioch and take responsibility for a collection on behalf of Jerusalem famine victims (11:19-30; 12:24-25). Meanwhile, Herod kills James the disciple of Jesus and imprisons Peter (12:1-5). Herod later incurs God’s wrath and dies a gruesome death (12:20-23).

Luke next reports the first missionary journey of Paul and Barnabas (13:1 – 14:28): they go to Cyprus and southeastern Asia Minor, preaching in synagogues but enjoying even greater success among gentiles. Paul strikes Elymas the magician blind, refuses worship when identified as a god, and is stoned and left for dead. The increasing number of gentile converts leads to a conference in Jerusalem at which James the brother of Jesus proposes terms for the inclusion of gentiles in the church (15:1-35).

Luke reports a second missionary journey of Paul and Silas (15:36 – 18:22). Here they travel through Asia Minor and what is now Greece (Macedonia and Achaia); Thessalonica, Beroea, and Corinth; and encounter much hostility, including an imprisonment interrupted by an earthquake at Philippi. This section of the book also includes an account of Paul preaching on the Areopagus to philosophers in Athens (17:16-34).

Luke then reports a third missionary journey of Paul (18:23–21:14): he travels through Asia Minor and Greece, visiting many places where he has been before, but Luke focuses his account on events in Ephesus. There, a powerful preacher, Apollos, is instructed by Priscilla and Aquila; Paul brings the gift of the Spirit to former disciples of John the Baptist; seven sons of the priest Sceva are mauled by an evil spirit; and Paul survives a riot after Demetrius the silversmith convinces people that the city’s economy and honour are threatened by the Christian affront to the Temple of Artemis. On the journey home, Paul preaches a fateful sermon in Troas (during which Eutychus falls out a window and must be miraculously revived), and he offers a farewell homily to the Ephesian elders in Miletus.

Luke devotes the last part of Acts to reporting on Paul’s life as a prisoner (21:15–28:31). Paul is arrested in Jerusalem (21:17-36) and subsequently moved, first to Caesarea and then to Rome. He gives his testimony repeatedly, before the Jewish populace (22:1-22), before the council (23:1-10), before the governor Felix (24:1-27), before a later governor Festus (25:1-12), and finally before Festus’ guests Agrippa and Bernice (25:13–26:32). The adventurous voyage to Rome involves a shipwreck on the island of Malta (27:1–28:10), but eventually Paul is brought

to Rome, where he is placed under house arrest but allowed to preach freely for two years (28:11-31).

Structure of Acts

Probably due to lack of clear division in the Acts narrative, very few authors have attempted a systematic structuring of the book of Acts. The following outline seems to do some justice to the progress of the story.

I.                       Introduction to the period of the Church 1:1-26

II.                    Mission in Jerusalem 2:1–5:42

III.                  The mission’s outward path from Jerusalem 6:1–15:35

IV.                 Paul’s path to Rome 15:36–28:31

There is a geographical aspect to the progress of the narrative as well. The movement starts in Jerusalem, shifts to Judea and Samaria, and finally reaches the ends of the earth – Rome.

Major Themes in Acts

God is in Control of History

The book of Acts emphasizes that God is sovereign over history: God determines what will happen, as well as when, where, and how it will happen. In both the Gospel of Luke and the book of Acts, God brings to pass events predicted in Scripture (Luke 1:20; 4:21; 21:24; 22:16; 24:44; Acts 1:16; 3:18; 13:27; 14:26). Times and seasons are set by God (Acts 1:7; 17:26), and God determines the fate, purpose, or destiny of people’s lives (Acts 2:23; 10:42; 13:47-48; 17:31; 22:10). One of Luke’s favourite words is the simple Greek term dei, which means “it is necessary”; he uses this word repeatedly to indicate that things must happen because God has willed them to happen. It was necessary for Jesus to die and rise from the dead (Acts 17:3; cf. Luke 9:22; 13:33; 17:25; 24:7,26); it was also necessary for Judas to be replaced (1:22), for Paul to visit Rome (19:21; 23:11; 25:10; 27:24), for the gospel to be proclaimed to Jews first (13:46), and for Christians to experience tribulations (14:22) and to suffer for Christ’s name (9:16). Curiously, Luke does not explain why such things are necessary, beyond the simple affirmation that they have been foretold in Scripture and are in accord with the will of God. Nevertheless, Luke apparently expects his readers to be comforted by the assurance that God is in charge and that everything is going according to plan.

Divine Guidance

Luke maintains that the God who is in charge of history offers divine guidance to those willing to submit to the predetermined plan. In the book of Acts, God directs people through the Holy Spirit (8:29,39; 10:19; 11:12; 13:2-4; 16:6-7; 19:21; 20:22-23,28), through prophets (11:28;

21:11), through angels (5:19; 8:26; 10:3,7,22; 11:13; 12:7-10,23; 27:23-24), and through visions (10:3,11-19; 11:5-10; 16:9-10)). Such guidance is evidence that the God who predestined what has happened up to now (2:23; 4:28) is still in control and sometimes will reveal to people what must happen next.

God Makes Promises and Keeps Them

As we have noted, Luke emphasizes that many of the events that he reports constitute the fulfilment of predictions and promises that God made in the Scriptures. The suffering of the Messiah (a difficult concept for Jews to grasp) should not have been unexpected, since it was foretold in Scripture (4:11,24-28; 8:32-35; 13:27,29; 17:3,11). In  addition,  Luke finds scriptural

promises for the resurrection of Jesus (2:25-28,34-36; 3:18; cf. Luke 18:31-33; 20:17; 22:37,69; 24:25,27,44), the outpouring of the Holy Spirit (2:16-21), and the ministry to gentiles (13:47; 15:15-17; 28:25-28; cf. Luke 24:45-47). The prior prediction of such events certainly proves that

the occurrences were part of God’s plan, but Luke may also have a broader purpose in mind: by demonstrating that God has been faithful to prior promises, he shows that God can be trusted to keep the promises that have not yet been fulfilled. The fact that Jesus had not returned as soon as many had expected may have prompted Luke to emphasize this theme: God has kept promises in the past and can be counted on to keep promises in the future.

God’s Faithfulness to Israel

One specific issue that Luke wants to address in Acts is the question of whether God has been faithful to the covenant people of Israel. The destruction of the temple in Jerusalem and the blunt fact that most Jews did not believe in Jesus as the Messiah must have raised questions in this regard: Has God abandoned the “chosen people”? Luke suggests the opposite: God has fulfilled all of the divine promises to Israel, but many individual Jews abandoned God and opted out of the benefits that the fulfilment of those promises would bring. In his Gospel, Luke maintains that God visited the people of Israel with salvation and peace (1:68,77-79), but tragically the people did not recognize the time of their visitation or the things that make for peace (19:41-44). Thus, according to Luke’s Gospel, many of God’s chosen people “rejected God’s purpose for themselves” (7:30).

So too in Acts, Peter insists on two things: (1) God has been faithful in sending Jesus first to Israel (3:22,26), and (2) those who do not listen to the one whom God has sent “will be utterly rooted out of the people” (3:23). The Paul of Acts also echoes these two points: the word of God is spoken first to the Jews, but sometimes they reject it and prove themselves “to be unworthy of eternal life” (13:46; cf. 18:6). Such language has a polemical edge to it, but Luke’s main point seems to be that God cannot be blamed for the disasters that befall Israel (including the physical disaster of the destruction of Jerusalem and what Luke considers to be the spiritual disaster of Jews mission out on the salvation brought by the Messiah). Such catastrophes are not due to any lack of divine fealty or initiative but rather are simply a consequence of the same rebelliousness that Moses and the prophets decried throughout the history of Israel (3:22-23; 7:48-53; 28:25- 28).

By the same token, however, the book of Acts insists that God’s faithfulness has indeed been effective for a core group within Israel, since large groups of Jews do not accept the gospel: three thousand in 2:41, five thousand in 4:4, and a great many more (including a “great many of the priests”) in 6:7. Eventually, Acts reports that there are “myriads” of Zealous believers among the Jews (21:20). For Luke, these Jewish Christians represent the “true remnant of Israel,” and he regards them as sufficient in number to constitute the covenant people of God for whom the ancient promises are faithfully fulfilled.

Mission to the Gentiles

While insisting that God has been faithful to Israel, the book of Acts clearly is interested also in celebrating and promoting the movement of Christianity into the gentile world. The author wants to make clear that this is no aberration: the salvation of the gentiles was prophesied in Scripture (2:17; 3:25; 13:47; 15:17; cf. Luke 2:32, 3:6), and the apostolic mission to the gentiles

was authorized by Jesus (1:8; 9:15; 22:21; 26:17; cf. Luke 24:47); further, it has been conducted in accord with the prompting and guidance of the Holy Spirit (10:44; 11:12,15; 15:8).

However, Luke does not present the gentile mission as some “Plan B” of God or that gentile mission was a consequence of Israel’s unfaithfulness (the idea known as “supersessionism”). Rather Luke presents the gentile mission as an outgrowth of Israel’s faithfulness and obedience to God: a righteous remnant of the Jewish people accepts their Messiah and act in accord with what he wanted them to do next, bringing salvation to all. Luke is careful to note that the church began as a completely Jewish, messianic movement within Israel and that “Christians” came along only later (11:26). He thinks that this is worth remembering.

Centrality of Jerusalem

The Jewish matrix for Christianity is preserved in Acts by a strong emphasis on the central role of Jerusalem. Luke established the importance of Jerusalem in his Gospel by presenting both Jesus and his disciples as devoted to the city and its temple (2:49; 13:33-35; 19:41-44; 24:52-53) and by structuring his Gospel so that much of the book is oriented toward Jerusalem as Jesus and his followers’ journey to that city. Now, in Acts, the reverse is true: the book is organized in such a way that everything proceeds out from Jerusalem. Jesus explicitly orders his disciples not to leave the city until they receive “the promise of the Father” (i.e., the Holy Spirit) (1:4), and after that happens, the mission of the church proceeds as he directed (1:8): first in Jerusalem, then in the broader area of Judea, then in Samaria, and finally to the rest of the world. Significantly, however, all missionaries – including Philip, Peter, and Paul – report back to Jerusalem, which seems to be “home base” in some spiritual sense even after its geographical importance has dwindled.

Lukan Generosity

As in Luke’s Gospel, the book of Acts continues emphasizing God’s acceptance of the poor, the marginalised, and any who might be considered outcasts. The early church is marked by a commitment to eliminating poverty (4:34) and by an inclusive vision that seeks to incorporate people from all nations, including such traditional enemies as Samaritans (8:4-25). In a broader sense, Luke is unusually generous in his portrayals of non-Christians; he seems intent on presenting almost all people, not just pitiable social outcasts but also potentially hostile unbelievers, in the best possible light. This applies most noticeably to Roman officials who, even

when they are not Christians themselves, often are presented as just and sympathetic in their dealings with the Christian missionaries (18:12-16; 19:35-41; 23:10-35). Similarly, idolaters in Athens are depicted as “extremely religious” people who possess a sincere desire for truth (17:22-23,32). Even the natives on the island of Malta are portrayed as kind and generous pagans who come to the aid of castaways (28:2,10). Non-believing Jews are treated somewhat more harshly in Acts, but certain Jews who don’t accept the Christian message are nevertheless presented as wise and responsible people (e.g., Gamaliel and those who heed his counsel [5:34- 39]). In addition, the Jews and their leaders are to be excused for killing Jesus, since they acted in ignorance (3:17; cf. Luke 23:34).

Jesus Absent and Present

The book of Acts begins with an account of the ascension of Jesus, emphasizing his physical absence from the earth. Although he sometimes appears to people on earth in visions (9:3-5), he is now located in heaven, at the right hand of God (3:20-21; 7:55-56). Thus, in some sense, the book of Acts is devoted to describing the circumstances and conditions of living for Christ during the time of his absence (cf. Luke 5:35) – that is, the interim between his ascension and his Parousia. The significance of this absence, however, is diminished by the fact that Jesus remains present in certain respects:

§   Through the Holy Spirit: In former times the Holy Spirit inspired Scripture to be written, predicting numerous events to which Luke refers in Acts (1:16; 4:25; 7:51-52; 28:25-26). Now the Holy Spirit empowers people to be witnesses for Jesus in word and deed (1:8; 4:8,31; 7:55), and the Holy Spirit directs the life and mission of the church (8:29; 10:19; 11:28; 13:2; 15:28; 16:6; 20:23; 21:4,11). The Spirit is described in very personal terms in Acts: people can lie to, test, or oppose the Spirit (5:3,9; 7:51). But the Spirit is also closely connected to Jesus, such that what the Holy Spirit does on earth is to be regarded as the continuing activity of Jesus. In 16:7, Spirit is referred to as “the Spirit of Jesus.”

§   Through the word: The “word of God” is personified in Acts as an active and powerful force, and readers are expected to regard what the word of God accomplishes on earth as another expression of the continuing activity of Jesus: the word grows, spreads, and increases (6:7; 12:24; 13:49; 19:20); it is sent to people for their salvation (13:26). In Acts, accepting or receiving the word of God is essentially the same thing as accepting Jesus or becoming a Christian (see 8:14; 11:1; cf. Luke 8:11).

§   Through the lives of his followers: Jesus identifies his continuing presence on earth with the activity of those who believe in him: when the apostles or other followers of Jesus teach, preach, and heal people, the reader is expected to realize that it is Jesus teaching, preaching, and healing people through them (see e.g., 9:34). Likewise, those who persecute the followers of Jesus are actually persecuting Jesus himself (9:5), for what is done to his followers is done to him.

§   Through the use of his name: in Acts, people are healed (3:6), receive salvation (4:12), forgiveness of sins (10:43), and other divine benefits by calling on the name of Jesus (22:16; cf. 2:21) and having faith in the name of Jesus (3:16). The name stands for the person: to praise the name of Jesus is to praise Jesus (19:17); to oppose the name is to oppose Jesus (26:9); to suffer for the name is to suffer for Jesus (5:41; 9:16). The one essential difference is that the name remains present and accessible in a way that the literal person does not.

Luke begins the book of Acts by noting that in his first book (Gospel of Luke) he wrote about what Jesus said and did “until the day when he was taken up to heaven” (1:2). Thus, his second book continues that account by relating what Jesus has said and done since the day he was taken up: it relates what Jesus continues to say and do through the Holy Spirit, through the word of God, through the lives of his followers, and through the appropriate use of his name.

Salvation

As in the Gospel of Luke, the book of Acts emphasizes Jesus’ identity as Saviour (Acts 5:31; 13:23; cf. Luke 2:11). Salvation is bestowed by Jesus (2:33-40; 5:31; 13:23-39; 15:1-11; 16:30-31)  and received through his name (2:21; 3:16; 4:12). Acts affirms that God has made Jesus to be Lord and Christ (2:36) and authorized him to grant salvation to any whom he chooses to save. The content of this salvation is twofold. Eventually it will consist of “times of refreshing” (3:20) and “eternal life” (13:46) when Jesus returns to restore (3:19-21) and judge (17:30-31) all humanity (10:42). Right now, however, being saved might mean receiving forgiveness of sins (2:38; 5:31; 13:38-39), or the gift of the Holy Spirit (2:38; 11:15), or healing (4:9; 14:9-10), or simply being rescued from some sort of temporal distress (7:25; 27:22-24,34,44) – this is not always clear in English Bibles, where the Greek words for “saved” or “salvation” are sometimes translated as other expressions (e.g., 4:9 where sesōtai, “has been saved,” is translated as “has been healed” in the NRSV). As in his Gospel, Luke’s focus in Acts tends to be on accessing the saving power of God in this present world so that people might be liberated from whatever prevents them from experiencing life as God intends.

Charismatic Manifestations of the Spirit

The book of Acts speaks more about the Holy Spirit than does any other book of the Bible, making more than seventy references to the Spirit. In fact, Acts is often referred to as the "Acts of the Holy Spirit" or occasionally as the "Gospel of the Holy Spirit". The frequent mention of the person of the Spirit and his role in the advancement of the Christian kerygma is obviously the reason behind the attribution of such epithets to the book. It is therefore surprising that little attention is paid to the quiet influence of the Spirit on People’s lives. We hear nothing about the Spirit’s role in generating faith or in effecting an inner purification of believers: no one is “washed in the Spirit” (1 Cor. 6:11) or “sealed with the Spirit” (Eph. 4:30), nor is any reference made to the fruit of the Spirit that affects the moral character of individuals (Gal. 5:22-23) or to

the unifying effect of the Spirit that binds all believers together into one body (1 Cor. 6:17; 12:13; Eph. 4:4; Phil. 1:27; 1 Pet. 3:8). The emphasis, rather, is on power and on external manifestations.

In Acts, people sometimes are said to be “filled with the Holy Spirit,” and this typically occurs in a dramatic fashion that transforms their status with God and/or imbues them with power to become witnesses for Jesus (2:4; 4:31; 8:14-17; 10:44-48; 19:1-7; cf. 1:8; Luke 24:49). No exact pattern or set of qualifications for receiving the Spirit can be discerned: the gift of the Spirit is variously linked to prayer (4:31; 8:15; cf. Luke 11:13), the laying on of hands (8:17-18; 19:6), preaching (10:44), and baptism (2:38; 19:2-6; but see 8:15-16; 10:47). Sometimes those who are filled with the Spirit respond by speaking in tongues and prophesying (2:4-11; 10:45-46; 19:6). The book of Acts also emphasizes the performance of “signs and wonders,” spectacular miracles wrought by those who receive the Spirit’s power (2:43; 4:30; 5:12; 6:8; 14:3; 15:12). Such actions place the apostles and other followers of Jesus in line with heroes of God such as Moses (7:36) and Jesus himself (2:22); they serve to authenticate the word of the gospel, proving that their bold proclamation is endorsed by spiritual powers not subject to human limitation – powers that usually are benevolent but that are not to be offended (5:1-11; 13:9-12).

Growth, Triumph, and Victorious Living

The book of Acts records the advance and progress of the church’s mission, with emphasis on its successful expansion and on its transforming effect on society. Luke repeatedly stresses that the church is increasing numerically (1:15; 2:41; 4:4; 5:14; 6:7; 9:31; 11:21,24; 12:24; 14:1; 16:5; 19:20; 28:30-31), as well as expanding geographically (1:8) and growing in terms of ethnic diversity (8:4-25; 10:44-48; 11:19-21). Christians become known in the empire as people who are “turning the world upside down” (17:6).

This motif of victorious transformation is also evident in the way that Acts depicts the lives of those who follow Jesus. In the Gospel of Luke, the apostles argued with one another over rank (9:46; 22:24); now in Acts, fellowship, goodwill, and mutual dependence on God seem to be the rule (2:44-45; 4:32-35). Previously Peter was a coward who denied that he even knew Jesus (Luke 22:54-62); now he boldly witnesses to Jesus in the face of persecution and death (Acts 4:8-12,19-20; 5:29-32).

In Luke’s Gospel, Jesus indicated that when the disciples we “fully qualified,” they would be like him, their teacher (6:40). In Acts, this appears to have been fulfilled: they are now like Jesus in word and deed. When disciples of Jesus speak in the book of Acts (but not in the Gospels), the reader is almost always expected to regard what they say as wise, godly, and true. Likewise, the disciples and other church leaders in the book of Acts regularly do the kinds of extraordinary things associated with Jesus in the Gospels: they cast out demons (8:7; 16:18; 19:12), heal the sick (3:6-7; 5:16; 8:7; 9:34; 14:8-10; 19:12; 28:8-9), raise the dead (9:40), know the secret thoughts of others (5:3), predict the future (20:29-30), discern the hidden but true meaning of Scripture (1:15-22; 2:16-22,25-32; 4:11,24-26; 8:32-35; 13:32-37), interact freely with angels

(5:19-20; 8:26; 12:7-10; 27:23-24), and even pass authoritative judgement on the wicked (5:1-10; 8:20-23; 13:9-11).

Just as people in Luke’s Gospel could be healed by merely touching the hem of Jesus’ garment (8:43-48), now in the book of Acts people can be healed by handkerchiefs or aprons that have touched Paul’s skin (19:12). Peter becomes so renowned for his healing powers that people bring the sick out to the street on cots, hoping that just his shadow may fall on them and make them well (5:15-16). Even the persecution of believers becomes an occasion for victory: when disciples are savagely beaten, they rejoice that they have been counted worthy of suffering for Jesus (5:41); when they are imprisoned, they sing hymns (16:25). Nothing, it seems, can dampen their spirits, or deter their confidence. And Luke shows again and again that this confidence is placed, as God sends angels (5:19; 12:7), earthquakes (16:26), or whatever else is necessary to rescue them and allow the mission to go forward.

Of course, there are also martyrs (7:54–8:1; 12:2), but their noble deaths do not impede the church’s overall progress and mission. Indeed, when Stephen (the first martyr) dies, he does so in a way that clearly echoes the passion of Jesus himself:

Jesus: “Father, forgive them; for they do not know what they are doing” (Luke 23:34).

Stephen: “Lord, do not hold this sin against them” (Acts 7:60) Jesus: “Father, into your hands I commend my spirit” (Luke 23:46). Stephen: “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit” (Acts 7:59).

Thus, in death – as in life – the followers of Jesus in the book of Acts demonstrate that they have become like their master.

PART II

CLOSE READING OF THE TEXT

For the close reading of the text, the division suggested by Raymond E Brown in his book ‘An Introduction to the New Testament’ is followed.

Introduction: Preparing Jesus’ Followers for the spirit (1:1-26)

The Prologue (1:1-2)

Luke begins the Acts of the Apostles as he had begun the Gospel of Luke, with a literary prologue which connects his two works (Gospel and Acts). The author explicitly refers to his “first account,” which in Greek literally means “first word.” Theophilus whose name means “lover of God” is the same recipient to whom Luke dedicated his Gospel, where he addressed him in a very respectful manner, “most excellent Theophilus” (Luke 1:3). He was like Luke’s patron who might have financed the investigation and writing of the two-volume work. He was probably a “Gentile God-fearer who may have become a “catechumen or Christian neophyte.” Although he is identified as the overt recipient, Luke’s intended audience certainly extends beyond his patron, addressing everyone who loves God. To refresh the memory of the reader, the author succinctly summarizes the contents of the “first account,” which deals with “all that Jesus began to do and teach until the day when he was taken up to heaven.” We are told that Jesus instructed “the apostles whom he had chosen through the Holy Spirit.” It is important to note that Jesus gives instructions through or by means of the Holy Spirit. Right from the start Luke highlights the central role of the Holy Spirt for the church’s mission. Just as the Gospel depicts Jesus’ whole life as “filled with the Holy Spirit” (Luke 1:35; 3:22; 4:14,18; 23:46), so Luke in Acts continues to show that it was the same Spirit that directs and guides the church’s missionary enterprise every step of the way.

From Resurrection to Ascension (1:3-11)

This scene depicts various events that happened between Jesus’ resurrection and his ascension. Luke begins his narrative proper with an account of appearances of the risen Christ to his followers (1:3); the promise of the Holy Spirit (1:4-5); the commissioning of the apostles (1:6-8); and the ascension of Jesus (1:9-11).

For “forty days” Jesus appeared to his disciples “by many proofs.” Forty is a conventional biblical number usually designating a period (years or days) of preparation for important future work; for example, it took forty days for Moses to be instructed in the Law (Exod. 34:28), Elijah to journey toward the mountain of God (1Kgs 19:8), and Jesus to be tested in the desert (Luke 4:2). The term “proof” refers to concrete evidence that substantiates without a doubt. These appearances during an extended period of forty days showed that Jesus was really “alive,”

convincing even those who had not expected Jesus to rise. To have seen the risen Christ was a qualification for being an apostle and a “witness” to him (Luke 24:48; Acts 1:22; 1 Cor 15:3-9). To prepare them for the difficult mission ahead, Jesus continued to instruct his followers about “the kingdom of God,” which was the central theme of Jesus’ preaching ministry.

The Greek word συναλιζόμενος (synaulizomenos) in verse 4 is literally translated as “eating salt with” and therefore suggests the recurrent theme of table fellowship found in Luke as well as in Acts. As Jesus engages the apostle in table fellowship, he commanded them to remain in Jerusalem and wait for the “promise of the Father.” Since Jerusalem was the city of Jesus’ destiny (Luke 9:51) and departure (Luke 24:50-53), the mission of the church will also begin there (Luke 24:47; Acts 1:8). The “promise of the Father” (Luke 24:49; Acts 1:4) is obviously the “baptism of the Holy Spirit” (Acts 1:5). While the Greek verb _baptizein_can simply mean “wash, soak, dunk, plunge (into water),” it has a deeper religious significance regarding purification rituals and washings. The promise of a baptism by a new Spirit filled with “power from on high” (Luke 24:49) is alluded to in several prophetic texts (Joel 2:28-32; Isa 32:15; 44:3; Ezek 36:25-26). Such a gift and activity of the Spirit was perceived to be the sign of the arrival of the Messiah and the inauguration of the new era of witnessing to God’s salvation of all people (Luke 3:6,15-16; Acts 1:8; 2:4,16-39). Thus, according to Luke, the bridge from the old period to the new era will be crossed when the Spirit of Jesus is poured out on the Jewish-believing community at Pentecost (2:1-4) and on the Gentile Cornelius and his household (10:34-45).

The apostles are to wait in Jerusalem for baptism with the Holy Spirit as promised by John the Baptist (Luke 3:16). In relation to the full coming of the kingdom, Jesus tells them, “It is not for you to know the times or seasons.” Countering many speculations about the end time, this firm answer was essential for the composition of Acts in the 80s: IF the end were coming immediately, it would not be sensible to write a book for future readers or to envision a mission that would reach the whole world.

Acts 1:6-8 is Jesus’ last will and testament, commissioning his disciples to perform a new task until he returns. During the interim period until Christ’s return, they are to focus mainly on the missionary task that will be entrusted to them. Through the power of the Spirit the disciples will become “witnesses” to what Jesus “did and taught” (Acts 1:1) and preach repentance for the forgiveness of sins to all nations (Luke 24:47-48; Acts 2:38-39). This spirit filled testimony will begin in Jerusalem and spread through Judea and Samaria and eventually end in Rome (chap. 28), the centre of an empire extending to the known ends of earth.

Having thus prepared his disciples for the future, Jesus is taken up to heaven (1:9-11), which Luke already recounted at the end of the Gospel (Luke 24:50-51). This double narration of Jesus’ ascension further validates the “many proofs” of his resurrection (1:3) and intensifies his role and status as “God’s Messiah” (236). Several features draw attention to the supernatural character of this event. The cloud is a traditional symbol of the heavenly glory or presence of God (Exod. 16:10; Luke 9:34-35; Rev 11:12). The two men in white garments recall the two men dressed in dazzling garb at the tomb (Luke 24:4). Their presence evokes the two-witness motif (Deut.

19:15) that their testimony is true and encourages the apostles to become multiple eyewitnesses of Jesus’ resurrection and ascension.

As Jesus was being “lifted up,” the apostles were completely transfixed at what they saw. Since they kept “looking or gazing intently” heavenward, the two men rebuked them (v. 11). The point is that the apostles should not be surprised at what they just witnessed and that to remain idly gazing at the sky as they await his return is not an appropriate response to Jesus’ glorious ascension. Being assured that Jesus will return in just the same manner he left, the church “right now” must not lose sight of its mission of witnessing to Jesus until his return. Since mission is the essence of the church, all Christians (not just Galileans or apostles) are to constantly engage in the worldwide and universal mission that Jesus has commissioned them to do. No one should remain inactive in the interim time before the Lord’s return.

Awaiting the Spirit; replacement of Judas (1:12-26)

The eleven apostles dutifully obeyed Jesus’ instruction, returning to Jerusalem from the mount of Olives to wait for the promised Spirit. With the apostles were “some women, and Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers (1:14). Mary the mother of Jesus, who was depicted as a model of discipleship in the Infancy Narratives (Luke 1–2), is present with the earliest community of believers. The inclusion of Jesus’ mother and other women recalls the importance of the role of women in Jesus’ ministry (Luke 8:2-3; 4:38-39; 10:38-42; 18:1-5; 21:1-4) and foreshadows their significant role in the church’s worldwide and universal mission. We then see the earliest Christian community of believers was devoted “with one accord to prayer” (some translations have ‘constantly’ in place of ‘with one accord’). The Greek term homothymadon is often translated as ‘one accord’ or ‘one mind’ which emphasize the remarkable harmony and unity of the early Christian family. The disciples were praying for the “promise of the Spirit” (1:5) to empower them to become witnesses of the risen Christ (1:8) and to bear the message of the kingdom of God (1:3) to the ends of the earth. Praying together often strengthens faith during trials, preserves unity in times of conflict, and fosters fortitude in moments of doubt. Prayer is a dominant theme in Luke-Acts. Just as Jesus turned to prayer at important moments of his life, his disciples will do likewise. Not surprisingly prayer is mentioned thirty-one times in Acts.

Peter then takes the initiative in reconstituting the Twelve, acting for the first time as the leader of the apostles and of the community of Jesus’ followers. Peter now does precisely what Jesus had told him to do – to strengthen his brothers and sisters (Luke 22:32). He will continue to play a leading role in the life of the Jerusalem church and the missionary outreach in Judea and Samaria until he is forced to leave Jerusalem because of the persecution by King Herod (12:17).

Peter recounts the fate of Judas (1:18-19) and fills in the gap of what was left unsaid in the Gospel (Luke 22:21-22) [Luke’s version of Judas’ death, as voiced by Peter differs from Matthew’s (27:3-10)]. The gruesome details fit the description of the death of a wicked person who is punished or judged by God (Cf. Herod Agrippa in Acts 12:23; Antiochus in 2 Macc 9:1- 18).

Peter states that “it is necessary” (dei) that Judas be replaced. The assembly prayed to the Lord for guidance in the selection. The simple prayer (vv.24-25) expresses complete reliance on God’s wisdom to provide the right replacement. They then cast lots, a traditional means to determine God’s will (Lev 16:8; Num 26:55-56; Josh 19:1-40; Jonah 1:7-8; Luke 23:34). It may be mentioned here that after the descent of the Holy Spirit, the practice of casting lots was no longer employed in Acts as a means to determine the will of God. However, in this occasion the place of Judas is filled by the selection of Matthias (1:21-26). Matthias will not be mentioned again but what is essential is that the number Twelve be complete. Israel of old had twelve patriarchs representing the twelve tribes. The story of the Israel renewed in Jesus can start with no fewer than twelve.

MISSION IN JERUSALEM (2:1–8:1a)

The Pentecost & Peter’s sermon (2:1-36)

The word “Pentecost” literally means “fiftieth,” which was actually the name of the Feast of Weeks. It was celebrated at the end of the seventh week or fifty days after Passover (Exod 23:16; Lev 23:15-21; Deut 16:9-12). Pentecost was a harvest festival, when the Jews thanked God for the gifts of the grain harvest that gradually evolved into a pilgrimage feast to Jerusalem. Probably, the festival of Pentecost became associated with the celebration of the giving of the law to Moses at Sinai in the first century CE.

The mighty wind and tongues of fire signal the presence of God (Exod 19:18; 24:17; 1 Kings 18:38; 19:11-13). The essential work of this Spirit is transforming eyewitnesses into “ministers of the word: (Luke 1:2): “They began to speak in other tongues as the Spirit gave them utterance” (Acts 2:4). Already in fact they are “witnesses to the end of the earth” (1:8), since Jews from all over the Diaspora had gathered in the city for the feast and hear them proclaim “the mighty acts of God.”

There are noticeable parallels between the experience of Jesus in the Gospel (Luke 3–4) and the experience of Peter and the church in Acts. In the Gospel, while he was praying the Holy Spirit descended on Jesus (Luke 3:21-22), and he then delivered a programmatic proclamation at Nazareth concerning the nature and purpose of his mission (Luke 4:16-30). In Acts 2, Peter too was baptized with the Holy Spirit and then preached a sermon that was programmatic in describing the authentic nature of Christian mission and ministry.

Peter begins his proclamation by quoting a prophecy which affirms the basic consistency of what God has done in Jesus Christ with what the God of Israel did for and promised to the people of the covenant. Then Peter turns to tell what God has done in Jesus: a brief summary of his mighty works, crucifixion, and resurrection, culminating in scriptural evidence that he was the Lord and Messiah (2:36). In a certain sense this concentration on Christology represents a change from Jesus’ own style as narrated in Luke’s Gospel. Jesus spoke about God’s kingdom

and its challenge to accepted values. But in Acts, the focus of proclamation is Jesus himself, as if one could not announce the kingdom without first telling of Jesus through whom the kingdom was made present. The fundamental gospel became centred on the Christological identity of the risen Jesus as Messiah and Son of God (see Rom 1:3-4).

Reception of the Message; Jerusalem Communal Life (2:37-47)

The crowds were “stunned” or “cut to the heart” at hearing Peter’s speech and asked, “what are we to do,” parallel to the way the crowds, tax collectors, and soldiers responded to John the Baptist’s preaching (Luke 3:10,12,14). Peter responded by offering three things that they must do: (a) Repent; (b) be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ; and (c) receive the promised gift of the Holy Spirit.

(a)   Repent: the verb ‘repent’ or the noun ‘repentance’ denotes a radical change of mind, action, and attitude. It is the appropriate response to the gospel message. The invitation is to turn from rejection to accepting Jesus as Lord and committing one’s life to him.

(b)   Baptism: The second invitation is to be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ. By immersing in water and confessing their new faith in his name, they are forgiven their sins. The Greek word for forgiveness (aphesis) literally means release from legal or moral obligation. It could also imply cancelation or remission of a debt in a commercial or financial sense. The phrase ‘in the name of Jesus’ occurs frequently and functions like a refrain in Acts. Believers are baptised, healed, exorcised, preached to, and saved in the name of Jesus Christ. Calling upon “the name” carries power and authority, however, it is not a magical formula that demonstrates arbitrary power for one’s own benefits as evidenced in Acts 19:13.

(c)    Reception of the Holy Spirit: The third element is the reception of the promised gift of the Holy Spirit to those who repent and are baptized. There seems to be no specific pattern of how and when the Holy Spirit is poured out in Acts. The coming of the Holy Spirit sometimes follows baptism (8:12-17); at other times it precedes baptism (10:44-48); occasionally, it comes after baptism in connection with the laying on of hands (9:17-18; 19:5-6). At yet other times, both evens happen simultaneously (2:38; 8:38-39). This variety of ways that the gift of the Holy Spirit is poured out shows that it is not magic; it cannot be manipulated by any set procedure or formula. Moreover, it is made available to all (2:39).

Acts 2:41 reports that about three thousand of those who heard Peter’s sermons met his demands and were baptised. It then proceeds to describe how they lived. A beautiful image of the ideal community is painted with five essential characteristics:

i.        The community is devoted to the “teaching of the apostles.” Sound instruction is an important element of the new community.

ii.      The community is devoted to “the communal life” or “the shared community life.” The Greek word koinōnia connotes a mutual sharing of materials or possession.

iii.    The community was devoted to “the breaking of the bread.” Initially it must have referred to the ordinary meals that the believers regularly shared in private homes (2:46), during which

they recalled the Pascal mystery of Jesus’ passion and death. As time went on, the ordinary meals became separated from the celebration of the Eucharist, whereby the phrase “the breaking of the bread” becomes synonymous with the Eucharist or the celebration of the Lord’s supper.

iv.    The community was devoted to “the prayers.” Probably they were praying the traditional prayers that Jews recited every day. Prayer is an important feature of communal life and the life of Christian discipleship.

v.      The communal life of the believers, including their ability to perform “wonders and signs,” generated a sense of “awe in everyone’s soul” in Jerusalem (2:43). In vv. 44-46, Luke gives a more extended explanation of the life of the earliest Christians, commenting on their harmonious unity that expresses itself in practical ways, namely, holding “all things in common.” The early Christians did everything in a spirit of joy, sincerity of heart, and praised God, which had a tremendous effect on the unbelievers. As more people came to believe in Jesus

Christ, the church continued to grow numerically (2:47).

Activity, Preaching, and Trials of the Apostles (3:1–5:42)

Healing of the Crippled Beggar (3:1-10): The summary statements in 2:43 (wonders done by the apostles) and 2:46 (daily Temple attendance) prepare the way for the dramatic account of the healing that takes place when Peter and John go up to the Temple (3:1-10). Jesus began his ministry by manifesting the healing power of God’s rule (kingdom) to the amazement of all (Luke 4:31-37); now we see that Peter and the apostles carry on the same work with the same power. The healing is “in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth” (Acts 3:6), i.e., worked through the power of the heavenly Christ, not through any self-sufficiency of the apostles. Luke uses seven action verbs to describe the exuberant joy of the crippled beggar who was healed: the man jumps up, stands, walks about, enters the temple, walks around, leaps up, and praises God (3:8). Luke’s description might allude to Isaiah’s sign of the arrival of the messianic age and the restoration of Zion: “Then shall the lame one leap like a deer” (Isa 35:6).

Peter’s Sermon in Solomon’s Portico (3:11-26): The Lucan account of Jesus’ ministry combined healings and words; here in a similar pattern Peter’s healing is followed by a sermon. Peter attributes the miraculous healing to the power of Jesus whom God has appointed to be the Messiah and faithful Servant. This is all part of God’s plan. The language of God’s initiatives pervades the entire speech: God glorified his Servant Jesus (v.13), raised him from the dead (v.15), fulfilled what had been announced beforehand by the prophets (v.18), sent the Messiah (v.20), raised up a prophet like Moses (v.22), made a covenant with their ancestors, raised up his Servant, sent him first to Israel (v.26), and will bless those who repent from their evil ways (v.26).

Peter’s speech is profoundly Christological, containing seven titles: “Servant” (vv.13,26); “Holy One” (v.14); “Righteous One” (v.14); “Author of Life” (v.15); “Prophet” (vv.22,23);

“Christ” (vv. 18,20); and “offspring of Abraham” (v.25). Three of these titles are worth a closer examination:

  • Jesus is designated as “Servant” and is understood to be “God’s Servant”. The phrase ‘servant of God’ appears in the OT, often referring to Moses (Josh 1:7,13; 9:24; 1Chro 6:49). The phrase could also apply to messianic figures (Ezek 34:23-24; 37:24-25; Zech 3:8). Probably, Peter also connected Jesus to the suffering Servant of Isaiah (52:13–53:12). The explicit connection with the prophetic writing demonstrates the continuity between Israel’s story and that of Jesus and strengthens Peter’s argument that Jesus is the promised Jewish Messiah.
  • Jesus is portrayed as the “Righteous One.” God is also called “the Righteous One” in the OT (Ps 129:4; Zeph 3:5; Dan 9:14). Isaiah links the word “righteous” with the suffering Servant of the Lord who “will bear their iniquities” (Isa 53:11). In the Gospel of Luke, centurion acknowledges Jesus as “a righteous man” (Luke 23:47). God has set him apart to accomplish God’s will and mission as the righteous Servant.
  • Peter proclaims that although having been put to a shameful death, Jesus is the “Author of Life.” There are four references to this term in the NT (Acts 3:15; 5:31; Heb 2:10; 12:2). The Greek word archēgos could be translated as “originator” or “author” or “leader” or “founder.”

The second half of Peter’s speech is deliberative rhetoric, convincing his fellow Israelites to “repent” and “turn to God” so that their “sins may be wiped out” (3:19). The Jews of Jerusalem delivered up and denied Jesus the servant of God in the presence of Pilate who had decided to release him (3:13 = Luke 23:16); they denied the Holy and Just One and asked for a murderer (3:14 = Luke 23:18-19,25): Barabbas). Yet they acted in ignorance (3:17 = Luke 23:34a) as did their rulers, and accordingly they are being offered this chance to change. In the face of the apostolic preaching, however, ignorance ceases to be an excuse, and change of mind/heart is necessary if they are to receive Jesus as the Messiah when he is sent back from heaven (Acts 3:19-21). Peter ends the speech on a positive note assuring that “all the families” of the earth are included in the promise of blessings that God made to Abraham (Gen 12:3). The story that follows in Acts will insist that many of the people did change but most of the Jewish leaders did not.

The apostolic preaching stirs up wrath and leads to arrest of Peter and John, and probably the healed beggar too (see 4:10,14). The charge is they were “teaching the people and proclaiming that in Jesus the resurrection of the dead” (4:2). The Sadducees, who were the members of the priestly aristocracy in Jerusalem did not believe in a resurrection (Luke 20:27; Acts 23:6-8), were greatly annoyed by the Christian kerygma. However, the preaching had its own success. The believers grow from 120 persons (1:15) to 3,000 persons (2:41) and now to 5,000 persons (4:4). This shows that the opposition of the Jewish authorities could not prevent the expansion of the word of God and the work of the church.

A meeting of the Sanhedrin consisting of rulers, elders, scribes, and chief priests is convened against them (4:5-6), just as a Sanhedrin of the elders of the people, and chief priest and scribes was convened against Jesus (Luke 22:66). The interrogators focus on the miracle, demanding,

“By what name did you do this?” This question prepares the stage for Peter to deliver another missionary sermon to the Jewish leaders and elders (4:8).

Peter is “filled with the Holy Spirit,” manifesting courage, understanding, and boldness. He reflects what Jesus had said about not worrying how or what to say before rulers and authorities because “the Holy Spirit will teach you at what moment what you should say” (Luke 12:12). Peter maintains that the healing miracle took place “in the name of Jesus Christ the Nazorean” (4:10). He further goes on to assert that Jesus is Israel’s promised Messiah whom they crucified and whom God raised from the dead.

Annoyed at the boldness of the religious proclamation of the apostles who were not formally educated in religious matters or the Law of Moses, the Sanhedrin authorities blusteringly cut short debate and arbitrarily order Peter and John not to speak in the name of Jesus (4:18). Less than two months before, Peter in the high priest’s house had denied Jesus three times; now before a series of chief priests he cannot be silent about Christ (4:19-20). Among the Gospels Luke alone (22:31-32) had Jesus pray that, although Satan desired to sift Peter and the others like wheat, Peter’s faith would not fail, and he would turn and strengthen his brethren. Here we see the prayer fulfilled as Peter and John emerge unyielding from the Sanhedrin. The apostles insist that they have no other option but to speak about “what we have seen and heard” (4:20). The apostles must speak about Jesus and bearing witness to Jesus is the fundamental task of apostleship, which all disciples are commissioned to do (1:8,22). After having threatened the apostles, the Jewish leaders had no choice but to release them. They cannot punish them because the people were glorifying God for the sign that had been performed. Luke seems to suggest that the leaders have lost their authority and effectiveness since the people are able to recognize the signs of God’s action while their leaders could not.

The Church’s Prayer (4:23-31): Having been set free, Peter and John went “to their own people” (4:23). The community’s reaction to the release of the two apostles and the report of their trial and persecution is one of praise and prayer. Luke continues to underscore the importance of prayer life for the earliest Christians. The prayer (4:24b-30) consists of three parts:

  • The first (v. 24) acclaims the sovereignty of God. The believers invoke God as the “Sovereign Lord,” who is the Creator of heaven, earth, sea, and everything in them. The description of God and God’s activity correspond to Ps 146:6.
  • The second part of the prayer (vv. 25-28) is the interpretation of the persecution by employing the Scriptures. The words of Ps 2 are quoted. Here, the believers interpret that the hostility of “the kings of the earth” and “the rulers” that David had spoken about in Ps. 2 is being fulfilled. Luke again emphasizes that Jesus’ death happened according to God’s plan and Herod and Pilate were mere instruments in God’s hands.
  • The third part of the prayer (vv. 29-30) represents the community’s petition. The believers do not ask to be spared from persecution or suffering nor for vengeance against their enemies; rather, they ask for courage to continue to speak the word with even greater “boldness.”

They also implore God to continue to produce “signs and wonders” through the name of God’s “holy Servant Jesus” (4:30).

Three divine manifestations are shown to prove that believers’ prayer has been heard: the place was shaken; they were filled with the Holy Spirit; and they continued to speak the word of God with boldness.

The Common Life of the Believers (4:32–5:11): To demonstrate that Jesus’ followers were of one heart and soul, a summary (4:32-35) emphasizes some of the same features as the earlier summary in 2:42-47, especially holding things in common. Two examples follow. The first involves Barnabas (4:36-37) who positively exemplify the spirit of sharing. The other example, involving Ananias and Sapphira (5:1-11), is negative and illustrates divine punishment of those violating the purity of the early community. (Compare Achan’s action in OT [Josh 7]; Satan entered into Judas to give Jesus over [Luke 22:3-4]; and now he has entered into Ananias to lie to the Holy Spirit [Acts 5:3]). The impurity is eradicated by the judgement of Peter that brings about the fatal action of God. In describing the fear produced by this intervention, Acts uses the term “church” for the first time (5:11).

The Growth of the Church & Second Confrontation with the Sanhedrin (5:12-42): The “signs and wonders” performed by Peter and the other apostles lead people to admire them (5:13). In addition, “believers in the Lord” are no longer just “Men (4:4) but “great numbers” of them include “women” (5:14). Luke underscores the gender inclusiveness of the early Christian community at this stage of its development. Peter does not even need to touch the sick. His shadow is efficacious enough to cure those who are sick and possessed by unclean spirits. This proves that Peter and the apostles are chosen leaders who are endowed with the Holy Spirit to continue the healing ministry of Jesus Christ. By their healing ministry they draw attention to the gospel message and produce a positive effect on the church’s mission. Consequently, the church continues to increase, stretching beyond the city limits of Jerusalem (5:16).

The apostles’ confrontation could be divided into four smaller units: (a) the arrest, escape, and recapture of the apostles (5:17-28); (b) Peter’s defence speech (5:29-32); Gamaliel’s intervention and speech (5:33-39); and (d) the apostles rejoicing after having suffered for Christ (5:40-42).

§   This time, a sect of Sadducees who had great influence over the Sanhedrin are most hostile towards the followers of Jesus. The reason for the apostles’ arrest and imprisonment is “envy” because of the increasing reputation and popularity of the apostles. The people are not the only ones on the side of the apostles but also God. God directly intervenes on behalf of the apostles by sending an “angel of the Lord” to open the doors of the jail and lead them out. The angel of the Lord tells the apostles to continue preaching in the temple concerning “the words about this life.” The apostles obey the directive of the angel and deliberately disregard the prohibition that the Sanhedrin had issued earlier (4:18) and boldly proclaim the good news of Jesus Christ and the salvation that only Jesus can provide. As a result, the apostles are arrested

again (and as with the arrest of Jesus [Luke 22:6], care has to be taken not to arouse the people [Acts 5:26]) and transferred to the Sanhedrin. The high priest himself interrogates, indicating the seriousness of the situation.

§   Peter expresses his defiance of the high priest with a memorable line: “We must obey God rather than human beings,” and then gives a speech that is both apologetic and kerygmatic – both defending their action and proclaiming the Christian message. According to Peter, the purpose of God allowing Jesus to die, rise and ascend into heaven (or “exalted to God’s right hand”) is “to grant Israel repentance and forgiveness of sins” (5:31). Peter ends his speech by asserting that “they are witnesses of these things” (5:32), and since they have faithfully obeyed Jesus’ command (1:8), they are now filled with the Holy Spirit, the reason for their refusal to stop speaking and teaching about Jesus.

§   The Sanhedrin is “infuriated” by what they heard and wants to kill the apostles (5:33). This tragic conclusion is prevented by the intervention of a Pharisee named Gamaliel. Gamaliel argues that the entire matter should be left in God’s hands (5:39) and that in time the situation will surely resolve itself. Gamaliel’s counsel to the Sanhedrin does not appear to be an apology or a public defense of the Christian movement. Rather, he offers a pragmatic solution to a movement that he believes is doomed to fail just like other previous revolutionary attempts. What he says in 5:39 about “fighting against God” is ironic because from Luke’s perspective, the Jesus movement is “from God” and therefore the Sanhedrin is indeed “fighting against God.” Having been persuaded by Gamaliel, the Sanhedrin has the apostles flogged, orders them not to speak of the name of Jesus, and then releases them (5:40). The episode ends with the apostles continuing to teach and preach the good news of Jesus as the Messiah, not only in the temple courts but also from house to house (5:42). According to Luke, the Sanhedrin and its leadership have lost touch with the people and thus become ineffective. A new leadership of Israel is needed and is emerging – namely, the followers of Jesus Christ.

The Hellenists: Toleration; Stephen’s Trial and Martyrdom (6:1_–_8:1a)

The removal of external threat did not mean that all was well. Suddenly after the picture of the church as being of one mind, Acts 6:1-6 tells about a hostile division among Jerusalem Christians. The dispute happens between two groups: the Hellenists, who are probably Diaspora Jews whose primary language is Greek, and the Hebrews, Aramaic speaking Jews born in Palestine. While distinction might be one of language, cultural differences could also be a factor in the conflict between these two groups.

The Twelve (this is the only instance the apostles are called the “Twelve” in Acts) convene the community to find a solution. They propose that the Hellenists should select among themselves seven reputable men (of good standing, full of Holy Spirit and of wisdom) whom the apostles can appoint to be in charge of the distribution of food. The Twelve do not impose but simply propose. When intercultural sensitivity and respect are considered in a decision-making process, that solution is much more acceptable and is more likely to be successful. While

resolving the issue, the Twelve also make sure that the preaching and teaching of the message of Jesus are not being neglected. The whole community is pleased with the proposal and thus seven male Hellenists are chosen.

Stephen’s Arrest (6:8-15): A summary (6:7) about the spread of the word of God and the conversion of priests sets the stage for a conflict centred on Stephen (6:8–8:1a). Stephen is engaged in more than ministry of the table, which consequently leads to his accusation, arrest, and interrogation before the Sanhedrin. Failing to best him in argument and debate, the Hellenists incite some people to charge him with blasphemy against Moses and God. The charge of blasphemy could result in severe punishment (Exod. 22:28; Lev 24:11-16). They arrange false witnesses to conjure up several specific allegations (6:13-14). However, Stephen remains bold and faithful. The members of the Sanhedrin see a change in Stephen’s appearance. The literally “fix their eyes on him” (as people did to Jesus in Luke 4:20) because “his face was like the face of an angel.” This description alludes to Moses’ shining face when he came down from Mount Sinai (Exod. 34:29-35) and emphasizes the presence of God in Stephen’s life and witness.

Stephen’s Speech (7:1-53): Stephen’s speech which contains 1,014 Greek words, is the longest speech and one of the most important and difficult speeches in Acts. Stephen mainly gives a selective overview of biblical history, highlighting three major figures (Abraham, Joseph, and Moses) and the pattern of Israel’s rebellious behaviour and rejection of God and God’s messengers, and ends with a harsh critique of the temple. It is one of the most artistic and effective illustrations of intertextuality found in the Bible. Nonetheless, Stephen’s account of Israel’s history is not without problems. His version of the story contains numerous details that differ from the biblical accounts. Fitzmyer claims that the speech is “a Lucan composition” which draws on the Hellenist Christian tradition, possibly from Antioch. Some scholars propose that it reflects a Samaritan background.

The last verses are astoundingly polemic from a prisoner in the dock, for Stephen accuses his hearers of giving over and murdering the just Jesus even as their fathers persecuted the prophets. Not surprisingly this accusation brings rage against Stephen to the boiling point, and he is cast out of the city and stoned to death (7:54-60).

The scene is truly significant, not only because Stephen is the first martyr, but also because the death of Stephen in Acts matches so closely the death of Jesus in Luke. Both accounts speak of the Son of Man at the right hand of God (Luke 22:69; Acts 7:56); both have a prayer for the forgiveness of those who are effecting this execution (Luke 23:34a; Acts 7:60); both have the dying figure commend his spirit heavenward (Luke 23:46; Acts 7:59). In the figure of Peter, Acts has shown continuity with Jesus’ ministry of healing and preaching; in the figure of Stephen Acts has shown continuity with Jesus’ death. And just as Jesus’ death was not the end because the apostles would receive his Spirit to carry on the work, the death of Stephen is not the end, for observing is a young man named Saul (7:58). He consents to the death (8:1a), but in God’s providence he will continue the work of Stephen.

MISSION IN SAMARIA AND JUDEA (8:1b–12:25)

In this section, Luke presents the church undertaking important transitions, not only a movement away from Jerusalem to new geographical areas but also an encounter with and acceptance of different ethnic people into the church, namely, the Samaritans, the Ethiopian official, and the Gentile Cornelius. Between the missions of Philip and Peter is the story of Saul’s dramatic encounter with the risen Christ. Saul’s call and conversion set the stage for the mission to the Gentiles and to the ends of the earth.

Dispersal from Jerusalem; Philip and Peter in Samaria (8:1b-25)

Luke now narrates the movement of the gospel away from Jerusalem to the regions of Judea and Samaria (and Syria), fulfilling the second part of Jesus’ prophecy and commission (1:8). We have heard witness borne in Jerusalem culminating with the martyrdom of Stephen; now we are to hear preaching in the next two regions as the Hellenists are scattered throughout Judea and Samaria (8:1b; 9:31). The persecution was launched at first against the apostles by Jerusalem leaders (4:1,5-6; 5:17), then by Diaspora Jews against Stephen (6:9,12; 7:58), and now by Saul against all Christians (8:1,3). That stress gives rise to missionary endeavour (8:5-8), the testimony that Philip bears about the Messiah in Samaria.

Another of the Seven, Philip, appointed “to wait on tables” (6:2,6), turns out to be a preacher, the evangelist of Samaria, in a way that resembles Stephen and his testimony in Jerusalem. This shows that other than the Twelve become involved in bearing testimony to Christ. Philip not only successfully evangelizes Samaria, but he also works wonders of the sort already ascribed to apostles (2:43; 4:30; 5:12).

Philip encounters Simon who is skilled in magic (often called Simon Magus). Simon had fascinated the people of Samaria for some time before Philip arrives on the scene; but when he sees what Philip is accomplishing through “the name of Jesus Christ,” he too submits to baptism, becomes a Christian, and follows Philip. The testimony of Philip in Samaria becomes an occasion for sending two apostles, Peter, and John, to the people there; and when Peter and John laid their hand on them, they received the Holy Spirit (8:17). This episode also brings out the Lucan teaching that the gift of the Spirit comes only through the apostles (or, in time, through those sent forth by them; cf. 10:44-48; 18:25-27; 19:2-6; 20:29-30). It also tells of the incorporation of splinter groups into the mainstream church; Samaritans who are baptized become fully Christian by such an apostolic relationship.

Simon the magician is confronted by Peter when the former offers to buy the power to confer the Spirit. Simon probably made his offer because in the Greek world of the time pagans could buy a role as priests in various religions; it was often sold to the highest bidder. This spiritual gift, however, cannot be bought with silver or gold (recall 3:6). It is not for sale, and so Peter reacts to the offer of Simon: “May your money perish with you! You think that you can buy

God’s gift with money” (8:20). From this encounter Simon learns his lesson, and the episode ends with his repentance and the mention of the continued evangelization of Samaria.

Philip and the Ethiopian Eunuch En Route to Gaza (8:26-40)

After the evangelization of Samaria to the north of Jerusalem, Philip is now told to turn his attention to the south – to the road that goes from Jerusalem to Gaza. Philip was directed to meet the Ethiopian eunuch. Luke gives us a fair amount of information about the man from Ethiopia. His skin was probably dark. He was a court officer and treasurer of the queen, and a person of some means for he owns a “chariot” (8:28) and possesses a scroll of Isaiah that he probably just bought in Jerusalem. Since he was sitting and reading, he probably had his own driver. To be able to read makes him well educated. He is reading Isaiah, and the Hellenist Philip’s ability to interpret the prophet in order to explain Christ seems like a continuation of the risen Jesus’ interpretating the Scriptures for his disciples (Luke 24:27,44-45). Moreover, the man was “eunuch,” which means that he was probably castrated and therefore was prevented from entering the inner court of the temple in Jerusalem (Deut. 23:1; Lev 21:20). However, Philip has no hesitation about meeting the eunuch’s request to be baptized into the community of the renewed Israel. (Was Philip acting out the eschatological benevolence of Isa 56:3-5 towards eunuchs?) This openness prepares us for the admission of Gentiles, and by way of transition Acts stops here to tell us about Saul/Paul who would be the great emissary to the Gentiles.

The Commission and Conversion of Paul (9:1-30)

Acts 9 marks an important narrative transition in the plot of Acts. Luke tells the story of an ardent persecutor of Christian movement who became a valiant witness after having experienced a “Christophany,” a manifestation or revelation of the risen and exalted Jesus Christ. Luke has already introduced to the reader the threats of Saul (7:58; 8:1-3) who will later be called Paul (13:9). Now, Luke relates Saul’s dramatic turnaround or “conversion,” perhaps not so much as an abandonment of the God of Israel but more so a change from a Pharisaic persecutor to becoming a chosen vessel selected by Christ himself to bear witness on his behalf. Saul’s encounter of the risen Jesus on the way to Damascus may be categorised as a commissioning or call story that contains features resembling call narratives of OT prophets, except that in the case of Saul it involves a radical conversion. The significance of Saul’s commission and conversion is apparent since Luke will narrate it two more times (22:1-16; 26:9-18) from Paul’s lips in his speeches of self defense in order to add more details and nuances to this amazing encounter that a single account cannot provide. Structurally, Acts 9:1-30 may be divided into three distinct units: Saul’s encounter with the risen Christ (9:1-9); Ananias’ vision that leads to the healing and baptism of Saul (9:10-19); and Saul’s preaching in Damascus and Jerusalem (9:20-30).

Saul’s Encounter with the Risen Christ (9:1-9): Saul was determined to suppress the movement, going as far as Damascus to arrest both men and women and to take them back to Jerusalem for interrogation and punishment. Damascus was an important commercial city about 135 miles north-northeast of Jerusalem. It is situated along the main trade route connecting Mesopotamia and Egypt. Although Damascus was heavily influenced by Greek culture, it had a substantial Jewish population with several synagogues. It is possible that some of the believers who fled from Jerusalem after Stephen’s execution (8:1) had sought shelter in Damascus, as likely in the case of Ananias (9:10).

As Saul was approaching Damascus, he suddenly saw a light from heaven flashing around him (9:3). Description of flashing light from heaven often appears in biblical theophanies (Exod. 19:16; 2 Sam 22:15; Ezek. 1:4,13,26-28). The element of surprise underscores that this vision is completely initiated by God. The intensity of the light might have caused Saul to fall to the ground. Then Saul hears a voice saying to him, “Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?” Saul does not yet know who is speaking, so he asks, “Who are you, Lord?” Saul’s use of Kyrie (Lord) indicates that it is not just an expression of high respect but rather a recognition of the presence of the Divine. Ironically here Paul unknowingly makes a Christological confession. Jesus’ reply, “I am Jesus whom you are persecuting,” indicates that to persecute the way or his followers is to persecute Jesus himself. When Saul got up from the ground, he realized that he could no longer see even though his eyes were open. Saul had to be escorted by the hand into Damascus. For three days he was blind and did not eat or drink anything. The description of Saul now being completely helpless as a child (9:8-9) is quite contrast to the image of the ferocious young man whose name alone brought fear and terror to the believers (9:1,13,26). Prior to his encounter with the Lord, Saul was seeking to put an end to those who were following the “way,” but now he cannot find his own way without the help of others.

Ananias Heals and Baptizes Saul (9:10-19): Ananias is called a “disciple,” which is a designation for a Christian believer or follower (6:1,2; 9:1,10,19). Paul later describes Ananias as “a devout observer of the law, and highly spoken of by all the Jews” (22:12). Ananias’ response “Here I am, Lord,” indicates that he recognizes the Lord Jesus in the vision and is ready to carry out his will. Ananias is further informed that Saul is praying implying that he poses no threat, and that he is expecting him. While Ananias is having a vision about Saul, Saul is having a vision about Ananias which underscore God’s direct involvement. Ananias is hesitant but the Lord commands him to go (9:15). Jesus then delineates the threefold purpose of Saul’s call and conversion:

i.        Saul is Jesus’ “chosen instrument” to carry out God’s will (13:46-47; 20:24; 22:14; 26:16). It is not surprise that Paul later kept insisting that he is rightfully an “apostle,” who is chosen and sent by Jesus (Rom 1:1; 1 Cor 15:3-11).

ii.      As Jesus’ instrument or “vessel” (Rom 9:20-24; 2 Cor 4:7), Saul acts as Jesus’ emissary by taking the message to both Jews and Gentiles (13:46-47; 20:21; 22:15; 22:18-21; 26:16-17).

iii.    Saul will suffer as he bears witness to Jesus’ name (13:46; 20:19,23; 22:18; 26:17). Suffering was an integral part of Paul’s missionary endeavours as also attested in his letters (1 Cor 4:11-13; 2Cor 11:23-33; Rom 8:25; Phil 4:12-13).

Saul who acted as a representative of the high priest to persecute those who called on Jesus’ name is now a chosen vessel to confess and suffer on behalf of that name. Saul’s life is radically changed and transformed.

Ananias follows the Lord’s instruction and upon entering the house lays hands on Saul and calls him “my brother” (9:17) acknowledging that Saul has been accepted into the community as a fellow believer. Ananias then assures Saul that it was the “Lord Jesus” whom he had seen on the road and who has sent him [Ananias] to come to lay hands on him so that he may regain his sight and be filled with the Holy Spirit. The healing is immediate, and Saul is then baptized and given food to eat in order to regain strength.

Saul’s Preaching in Damascus and Jerusalem (9:20-30): After several days of staying with the disciples, Saul begins preaching in the synagogues in Damascus, proclaiming that Jesus is the “Son of God” (9:20). Besides, he also uses convincing arguments to prove that Jesus is Israel’s promised Messiah (9:22). Saul’s bold testimony and proclamation in Damascus cause great bewilderment since they have not forgotten Saul’s ruthless persecution of those invoking Jesus’ name (9:22). The completely unexpected turn of events caught them off guard.

Luke relates that “after some time had passed” (9:23) the Jews in Damascus conspired to kill Saul. They tried to catch him, but his disciples helped him to escape. The allusion to “his disciples” indicated that Saul had been in Damascus for a long enough time to have a group of followers to assist him to foil the plot in a daring getaway. This also points outs that Saul’s life was in grave danger in Damascus because of the proclamation of the gospel and a quick escape from there was necessary.

Three years had passed since Saul went to Damascus to arrest the followers of Jesus. The news of his return to Jerusalem as a transforming disciple of Jesus Christ was a bit suspicious for the “disciples” (9:26) to say the least. Their fear of Saul had not completely dissipated. But through the intervention of Barnabas, the apostles were put at ease and became convinced that the Lord had really appeared and spoke to Saul. Just as Saul had “boldly preached the name of Jesus” in Damascus, he now fearlessly and freely preaches in Jerusalem (9:27-28). Similar to what had happened in Damascus, the “Greek speaking Jews” plotted to kill Saul and so the believers had to rescue him, taking him first to Caesarea and then sent him off to Tarsus (9:30). From this moment on Saul disappears from the scene only to re-emerge from the same location at Tarsus in Acts 11:25.

Peter at Lydda, Joppa, Caesarea, and Back to Jerusalem (9:31–11:18)

When the church began spreading to Judea and Samaria, the Hellenists and Saul took centre stage (with Peter invoked chiefly to face Simon Magus). Now, however, with the church at peace

(9:31 – a transitional summary), Peter returns to the fore. Peter continues to perform miraculous healings and boldly preaches the good news along the coastal cities of Lydda, Joppa, and Caesarea to Jews as well as Gentiles.

Peter Heals Aeneas and Raises Tabitha (9:31-43): Peter’s cure of Aeneas at Lydda (9:32-35) with the command to rise echoes closely Jesus’ cure of the paralyzed man (Luke 5:24-26). Even more closely Peter’s revivification of Tabitha at Joppa (9:36-43) resembles Jesus’ action in raising the daughter of Jairus (Luke 8:49-56). No power has been withheld from the church, not even the power over death itself. What Peter does next will start a chain of actions that will eventually take Christianity outside Judaism to Gentiles and to Rome, the representative of the ends of the earth.

The Conversion of Peter and Cornelius (10:1-48): Cornelius is not just any soldier but a Roman centurion with a hundred men under his command. He is a “Gentile” and a “foreigner” who is “uncircumcised.” And yet he is introduced as a model Gentile with deep religious commitment. He was probably one of those Gentile faith-seekers or synagogue-adherents who was attracted to Judaism, who had not become a full convert. Cornelius’ vision (10:3-6) contains several important elements to underscore God’s initiative and active involvement in the conversion of the first Gentile in Acts. This vision happened in the afternoon at about three o’clock. The time reference indicates that he was not dreaming or asleep but fully awake. Cornelius “clearly” saw an angel and heard his name; he stared intently “in fear” and addressed the heavenly figure with respect, calling him “Lord.” The angel assures Cornelius that God has heard his prayers and accepted his piety and devotion as a “memorial offering.” Cornelius’ piety is repeatedly emphasized (10:2,4,22,31,35). The angel gives Cornelius details to help find Simon who is called Peter (10:5-6) and Cornelius swiftly carries out the angel’s directives.

The next day around noon Peter falls into a “trance,” which probably means “an ecstatic experience in which one ‘stands outside’ oneself while awake. It occurs as Peter is praying (10:9; 11:5). Peter’s vision is described in 10:11-16. All the animals in the sheet, are considered unclean and therefore prohibited for eating (Lev 11; Deut. 14). Despite Peter’s strong opposition, the heavenly voice declares three times that Peter should not call “profane” what God has made clean. The object being lifted back up to heaven into the presence of God demonstrates to Peter that there is nothing in it that is unclean and could not be eaten.

While Peter is still pondering the meaning of the vision, the messengers arrive. The Spirit informs Peter about the arrival and directs him to go accompany them “without hesitation.” To ease Peter’s doubts the Spirit makes a claim saying, “I have sent them,” and Peter obeys. The report in 10:22 is the second retelling of Cornelius’ vision. The most important detail is that Cornelius had received a visit from a holy angel directing him to send for Peter to go to Cornelius’ house in order to hear what Peter has to say. This implies that Cornelius is waiting to hear the “message” that Peter has been proclaiming concerning Jesus as Israel’s Messiah and Saviour. Peter then invites the three strangers into the house and shows them hospitality

(10:23a). By doing so, Peter on the one hand obeys the directive of the Spirit but on the other hand is breaking the law of purity.

The next day the three messengers accompany Peter and “some of the brothers” from Joppa to Caesarea. These six brothers (cf. 11:12) will be important witnesses to what occurred in the house of Cornelius (10:45). Cornelius has been anticipating Peter’s arrival and had even gathered his relatives and close friends. Upon meeting Peter, Cornelius prostrates himself before Peter and pays him homage. Peter quickly rejects Cornelius’ obeisance as he is only a human being. Peter states that it is “unlawful for a Jewish man to associate with, or visit, a Gentile” (10:28) but God had told him not to call anyone “profane or unclean” and he now fully understands the meaning and significance of his vision. Now, Peter sees clearly that the issue at stake was not just food but people. Peter’s declaration of the abolition of a distinction between Jews and Gentiles is a clear indication of a personal conversion. Peter now wants to hear directly from Cornelius as to why he was summoned. Cornelius responds by giving his own version of the story (10:30-33). This retelling offers yet another reminder of God’s initiative and involvement in the conversion of the Gentiles.

Peter’s speech in 10:34-43 is the most concise summary of the apostolic kerygma. It affirms as well as expounds several new themes, the most important of which is the announcement of the good news of salvation in Jesus Christ available for all people. The speech may be divided into three parts: Introduction (10:34-36), apostolic kerygma (10:37-41), and conclusion (10:42-43). Peter begins with a solemn declaration: “God shows no partiality.” He now “truly” understands that God is impartial, showing no favouritism toward anyone or toward any nation in particular. As long as a person fears the Lord and acts in righteousness, that person is acceptable to God; and salvation through Jesus is offered not only to the people of Israel but also to Gentiles because Jesus the Christ is “Lord of all” (10:36b). The central part of Peter’s speech (10:37-41) gives a précis of the life and ministry of Jesus, one that follows the basic story line of the Gospel. Peter testifies to Jesus’ resurrection and claims that Jesus has been physically eating and drinking with him and the apostles. Peter concludes the sermon (10:42-43) by stating the commission of Jesus to preach to the people and bear witness to his name. Jesus’ role as “judge” of the living and the dead alludes to Daniel’s heavenly Son of Man to whom God gave the authority to carry out the final judgement (Dan 7:13-14; see also Luke 12:8; Acts 17:31). Peter finally claims that “all the prophets” have announced that everyone can obtain “forgiveness of sins” (2:38) through Jesus so long as they “believe in him,” an expression found only here in Luke-Acts.

The sudden outpouring of the Holy Spirit upon Cornelius and his household (10:44-48) brings the episode to its climax. The description of the “falling” of the Holy Spirit is very similar to that of Pentecost (2:1-4). The effect of the Holy Spirit, in particular the “speaking in tongue” and “extolling God” was so obvious that neither Peter nor the circumcised believers from Joppa could doubt or deny it. It was evident to them that God had accepted the Gentile Cornelius and his household. This experience transformed Peter and it will become an irrefutable case for Peter to convince the Jerusalem church to fully accept Gentiles and to integrate them into the Christian community without reservation or condition.

Peter’s Defense of Table Fellowship with Gentiles (11:1-18): as soon as Peter arrived in Jerusalem, he was immediately confronted by the “circumcised believers” or literally, “those from circumcision” (11:2). They were probably the same group that later demanded theat Gentile converts be circumcised (15:5; cf. Gal 2:12). The issue here in 11:3 however is not circumcision but the violation of food laws. Peter defends his case by recounting both his own vision and the vision of Cornelius. Peter shows that his action of going to the home of the Gentiles and eating with them was completely guided by God.

Peter’s speech is his personal version of the story. It adds some important details, but it is to a great extent, repetitive. Peter attests that God gave those Gentiles the same gift of the Holy Spirit that he gave “us” when “we” believed in the Lord Jesus Christ (11:17). For Peter, the Gentile Pentecost in Caesarea is identical to the Jewish Pentecost in Jerusalem. The same gift of the Holy Spirit that the Gentiles had received is the decisive factor for Peter to engage in table fellowship with the Gentiles. Moreover, since the Gentiles have been “immersed” in the Holy Spirit and in the water of baptism, they have become “clean,” and so close association with them is no longer considered ritually unclean. Peter ends his speech (11:18) with a rhetorical question, “Who was I to be able to hinder God?” Peter’s testimony leaves all stunned. At this stage the conflict between Jews and Gentiles seems to have been resolved and the Jews and Gentiles may come together in close fellowship. However, the Jewish-Gentile controversy, specifically concerning circumcision, is not fully resolved but only temporarily suspended until Acts 15.

Antioch; Jerusalem; Herod’s Persecution; Peter’s Departure (11:19–12:25)

Attention now switches from the church in Jerusalem to the church in Antioch, the capital of the province of Syria. At Antioch, the followers of Jesus were first called ‘Christians,’ the name by which they would be known for the rest of the time. This episode is divided into two main units: a report of the missionary outreach of the church at Antioch (11:19-30) and the story of Peter’s miraculous escape from prison (12:6-17), which is sandwiched between Herod’s persecution and death (12:1-5,18-25).

The Church at Antioch (11:19-30): The author now picks up the Hellenists’ story broken off in chap. 8 when they were scattered from Jerusalem to Samaria. Belatedly we are told that they went also to Phoenicia, Cyprus, and Antioch, preaching at first only to Jews but then gradually to Gentiles as well. Although a Hebrew Christian like Peter did accept a Gentile household into the community, seemingly the aggressive effort to convert Gentiles began with the Hellenists. When Jerusalem heard this, Barnabas was sent to Antioch to check on the development; and he approved it (11:22-23). This becomes the occasion of bringing to Antioch Saul. Thus, while the Jerusalem church in the person of Peter is taking the first steps toward admitting the few Gentiles, Antioch develops as a second great Christian centre, more vibrantly involved in mission.

11:27-30 introduces a new issue when a prophet named Agabus predicts that there would be a severe famine throughout the world. The response of the Antiochian church to send relief to their brothers and sisters in Jerusalem was swift and without hesitation. Each person contributed according to his or her ability. This gesture is more than just a material support from the church in Antioch to the church in Jerusalem. Rather it is “service” (12:25) that demonstrates genuine concern for the welfare of those who are less fortunate and affirms the bond of solidarity and partnership between the two churches among all believers.

Persecution and Death of Herod (12:1-25): This episode recounts various historical events that impacted the church in Jerusalem and throughout Judea. The main scene relates the story of Peter’s arrest and miraculous prison escape (12:3-17), which is placed between two incidents: the persecution of the church, including the execution of the apostle James (12:1-2) and the sudden death of King Herod (12:18-25), whose actual name is Marcus Julius Agrippa I (10BCE-44CE). He was the grandson of Herod the Great. The arrival of Agrippa onto the scene disrupts the peace and tranquillity of the church. He arrests some of the believers and has James executed. This new wave of persecution against the apostles and the church is more intense than on previous occasions (Acts 4:3; 5:18-19). During the Jewish Passover, Agrippa arrests and imprisons Peter, presumably to execute him after Passover (12:4). However, God intervenes through an angel to release him, even as God intervened by an angel to release him when he was arrested by the Sanhedrin (5:19). These divine interventions show God’s care for the great spokesmen of the gospel.

After the escape, Peter goes directly to the house of Mary, which fits the description of an early Christian “house-church,” and is a gathering space for prayer for many people (12:12) and a place of refuge for Peter. Mary is identified as the mother of John Mark. Mark is a cousin of Barnabas (Col 4:10) who accompanied Paul and Barnabas to Antioch and in their first missionary journey (13:5). Tradition ascribes to Mark the role of Peter’s interpreter whom Peter referred to as “my son” (1 Pet 5:13) and as the evangelist who wrote the earliest Gospel, which bears his name. Once Peter is allowed inside the house, he recounts the miraculous incident of his rescue and tells them to report everything to “James and to the believers” (12:17). After this, we are just told that Peter left and travelled to “another place” (12:17). However, Peter reappears in Jerusalem in Acts 15 to defend the Gentile mission and then disappears from the narrative of Acts.

The scene then shifts back to the prison and to King Agrippa (12:18-25). News of Peter’s escape causes no small commotion. However, without any success in finding out what had happened to Peter, Agrippa leaves Jerusalem to take up residence in Caesarea. There, he meets a painful death. Luke says that an angel “struck him down” because Agrippa accepted divine honours that should only be given to God. The expression “eaten by worms” could imply a terminal illness that involved immense pain and suffering. Ironically, the one who wanted to put an end to the followers of Jesus ends up dead while the movement continues to grow and gain

new members (12:24). The episode ends with a brief report of the success of the famine relief to Jerusalem. Barnabas and Saul return from Jerusalem to Antioch, accompanied by John Mark.

MISSION OF BARNABAS AND SAUL CONVERTING GENTILES; APPROVAL AT JERUSALEM (13:1 – 15:35)

The First Missionary Journey –Antioch Church sends Barnabas and Saul: Mission to Cyprus and SE Asia Minor (13:1–14:28)

This first missionary outreach finds Barnabas and Saul constantly on the move visiting many cities and towns proclaiming the word of God to both Jews and Gentiles. The episode can be broadly divided into five main units: (a) the commissioning of Barnabas and Paul (13:1-3); (b) the confrontation with the magician Bar-Jesus and the conversion of the proconsul Sergius Paulus in Paphos (13:4-12); (c) the mission at Pisidian Antioch (13:13-52); (d) the mission at Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe (14:1-20); and (e) the return to Antioch in Syria (14:21-28).

The Commissioning (13:1-3)

Luke describes how Barnabas and Saul are set apart for the missionary work by the church in Antioch at the explicit behest of the Spirit. Neither Barnabas nor Saul decides to undertake this mission on his own. They are chosen instruments of the Spirit. The emphasis is thus put on the role of the Spirit, which is behind the mission of Barnabas and Saul: they are “sent forth by the Holy Spirit” (13:4). The presence of “prophets” and “teachers” in Antiochian church shows that the church at Antioch is in good hands_._ They are attentive to the guidance of the Spirit (the role of the prophet) and is faithful to the apostolic teachings (the role of the teacher). Besides, the leadership team is ethnically diverse but his multi-ethnic church worships the same Lord who is Jesus Christ. The church supports Barnabas and Saul through fasting and prayer and empowers them through the laying of the hands to invoke God’s blessing and strength as they embark on a new missionary initiative.

Mission is Cyprus (13:4-12)

Along with John Mark, Barnabas and Saul go to Cyprus. Upon arrival, the three new missionaries seek out the synagogue to proclaim the word of God. After journeying through the whole island, they come to Paphos, the capital city and the seat of the Roman administration. Here, they meet the Roman proconsul Sergius Paulus, who was interested in the good news. However, a Jewish magician named Bar-Jesus who belonged to the proconsul’s entourage, tried to keep him away from the faith (13:8). Besides being a magician, he is also depicted as a “Jewish false prophet” (13:6). In the OT, a true prophet speaks and acts as God’s messenger while false

prophets only claimed to speak in God’s name but actually acted to their own advantage. Saul is now introduced also as Paul, who filled with the Holy Spirit, was able to perceive the true character of Bar-Jesus and exposed his idolatry and wickedness. Paul’s accusations against Bar- Jesus reveal much about his deceitful and diabolic character. Although he claimed to be “son of Jesus,” the Jesus that he associated with has no connection with the Jesus Paul preached. The blinding of Bar-Jesus, which takes effect immediately, exposes the magician as a fraud and is a clear proof of the illegitimacy of any claim to prophetic status. The scene ends with the conversion of Sergius Paulus who “believed” not by the punitive miracle that had happened but by the “teaching” about the Lord (13:12).

Mission in Pisidian Antioch (13:13-52)

The scene subtly introduces Paul as the emerging leader. The missionary team is now called “Paul and his companions” (13:13). They travel from Paphos to Perga in Pamphylia and gradually make their way to Pisidian Antioch, where they enter the synagogue of the Jews. The first major address of Paul in Acts is now recorded, being delivered in the synagogue of Pisidian Antioch (13:16b-41). It is addressed to diaspora Jews and Gentile sympathizers who are called “God- fearers” (13:16).

Paul’s speech in the synagogue in Pisidian Antioch (13:16-41) is structured by the threefold use of Andres Israelitai (Men, Israelites) or Andres adelphoi (Men, brothers), the form of address used in vv 16,26,38. Thus, it has three parts: (a) 16b-25; (b) 26-37; and (c) 38-41.

(a)   16b-25: The first part is a synopsis of the history of Israel, focusing on the promises that God had made as well as the times God had “to put up with” Israel. Paul emphasizes God’s guidance, which leads from the election of Israel to Jesus in a way that there is a continuity between Israel and the church.

(b)   26-37: The second part contains the central message of the Christian kerygma and the scriptural basis for its proclamation. The theme of Jewish ignorance and Christian testimony appear again. Paul says that the “message of salvation” that he announces “has been sent” by God. The proclamation deals with the death and resurrection of Jesus. Paul blames the Jewish rulers for their failure to recognize Jesus as Israel’s Messiah despite having heard prophetic texts read every Sabbath concerning him. Although Jesus was declared innocent (Luke 23:4,14-15,22) the leaders still handed him over to Pilate to be crucified. However, God “raised” him from the dead (13:30), and those who have seen him, have become “witnesses” of Jesus’ resurrection. In

vv. 32-37, Paul presents the Scripture testimonies of Jesus’ resurrection.

(c)   38-41: The third part of the sermon is an appeal to accept the promise and God’s offer of forgiveness in order to avoid retribution. Paul makes an urgent and unequivocal appeal for repentance in order to receive the “forgiveness of sins.” This offer is now made possible through Jesus Christ and not by the Law of Moses. He concludes his appeal with a stern warning by quoting Habakkuk 1:5. He warns his listeners not to scoff at the message that they had just heard.

The consequences of their ridicule and refusal will be disastrous because what the prophet said concerning a devastating judgement would happen to them.

The final scene of this unit (13:42-52) recounts the response of the people in Pisidian Antioch. The audience in the synagogue reacted positively to Paul’s sermon and desired to hear more of it the following Sabbath. Many Jews and proselytes followed Paul and Barnabas. However, the initial positive response turned sour on the next Sabbath. Because of the missionary success of Paul and Barnabas, the Jews opposed Paul’s message and teaching and slandered him (13:45). Therefore, Paul now “turn to the Gentiles” (13:46). This does not mean he has completely given up on the Jewish people. He will continue to proclaim the good news of Jesus as Israel’s Messiah to his fellow Jews whenever the opportunity arises. Seeking out the synagogues or going first to the Jews is the pattern of Paul in Acts (13:14; 14:1; 16:13; 17:1; 18:4,19; 19:8). At the same time, reaching out to the Gentiles is not an afterthought or because of the rejection by the Jews. Rather, it is in accordance with Jesus’ commission to go and bring salvation “to the ends of the earth” (1:8). Moreover, the mission of Paul and Barnabas fulfils God’s commission to his servant in Isaiah 49:6, becoming “a light for the Gentiles.” The Gentiles rejoiced at the good news of salvation, but the opponents of Paul and Barnabas mobilized some powerful men and women to persecute and expel the missionaries from the region. Although rejected and persecuted, Paul and Barnabas were not deflated but filled “with joy and the Holy Spirit” (13:52).

Mission in Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe (14:1-20)

Paul and Barnabas proceeded to Iconium (14:1-7) which was a prosperous commercial city, known especially for its plantations and wool industry. Despite their declaration of turning to the Gentiles (13:46), they resumed their preaching in the synagogues of the Jews (14:1). They experienced similar results of both success and failure. In spite of great opposition, Paul and Barnabas did not abandon their post but stayed “for a considerable time, speaking out boldly for the Lord” (14:3). Paul and Barnabas are now called “apostles” (14:4), title which Luke normally reserved for the Twelve (Luke 6:13; Acts 1:2,21-26). But since these two missionaries were “called” and “sent out” (13:2-3) to bear witness to Jesus Christ to the ends of earth (1:8), they are rightfully considered “apostles.” When Paul and Barnabas discovered that their opponents were plotting to mistreat and stone them (14:6), they fled to Lystra.

In Lystra, Paul and Barnabas continue their evangelization. Their ministry includes a miracle wrought for a man crippled from birth. The miraculous healing resembles both the stories of Peter’s healing of a man who was paralyzed in Acts 3:2-8 and Jesus’ in Luke 5:17-25. Having the ability to do what both Peter and Jesus were able to do, strengthen the perception of Paul’s authority and credentials as an apostle. The miraculous cure incites the pagan populace there to regard Paul and Barnabas as gods. To prevent the people and their priest from sacrificing to them, Paul utters a short discourse in which he tries to turn the people to acknowledge “the living God.” Unlike his address to Jews of Pisidian Antioch where he began his address with the

patriarchs of Israel, he now begins with creation. He teaches them that the living God is the creator and provider of all that is good for human life. This God sends them rain, seasons of fruitfulness, food and all that gladdens the heart. However, the people of Lystra were not convinced by Paul’s speech to turn away from idols to accept the one true and living God. They still wanted to offer sacrifices in their honour (14:18). When the Jews from Pisidian Antioch arrived, they provoked the people at Lystra and convinced them to attack and even stone Paul. Paul’s life, however, was saved by the disciples. The next day, Paul and Barnabas left for Derbe.

The Return to Antioch in Syria (14:21-28)

Paul and Barnabas continue to make many disciples and then retrace their steps, returning to the cities of Lystra, Iconium, and Pisidian Antioch. This return trip shows that the missionaries do not only seek to establish churches but to nurture them as well. Three caring features are mentioned (vv. 22-23). First, they encourage and strengthen the newly founded communities “to continue in the faith.” Second, the missionaries warn the believers of the imminent trials and tribulations, exhorting them to persevere through many hardships to enter the kingdom of God. And third, they appoint “elders” as local leaders in each church that had been established. Once back in Antioch, Paul and Barnabas call the community together to report everything that God had done through them and how God had opened the “door of faith” to the Gentiles. The emphasis here is on God’s doing rather than the accomplishments of the missionaries.

The Jerusalem Council (15:1-35)

Acts 15 is often identified as a watershed moment in Luke-Acts. Some authors even propose that this chapter is arranged both structurally and theologically at the very centre of Acts, making the Jerusalem Council a climax and unifying point of the entire narrative. The council made an enormous historical breakthrough by ratifying the Gentile mission without the need for Gentile converts to be circumcised. It also establishes faith in Jesus Christ and not the observance of the Mosaic law as the basis for salvation for both Jews and Gentiles. Implicitly the council decided that the following of Jesus would soon move beyond Judaism and become a separate religion reaching to the ends of the earth. Acts 15:1-35 is divided into three main scenes: (a) the convocation of the council to settle the controversy (15:1-5); (b) the debate and resolution of the conflict (15:6-29); and (c) delivering the letter and its aftermath (15:30-35).

The Convocation (15:1-5)

The peace that reigned in the church of Antioch is eventually disturbed, this time by Christians of Jewish background who come from Judea to Antioch and insist on the circumcision of Gentile Christians and on their having to observe the Mosaic law. In fact, they insist on these matters as necessary for salvation. Thus, some Antiochian Christians were opposed to the view

of Paul and Barnabas that Gentile Christians have no need to submit to circumcision or observe the Mosaic law. Unable to resolve the conflict among themselves, Paul and Barnabas had to go to Jerusalem to settle once and for all the issue of Gentile circumcision with the apostles and elders. During the intense debate, Peter stood up and intervened.

The Debate and Resolution (15:6-29)

Peter begins his speech (15:7-11) by recalling an event which happened “in the early days” of the church. The expression “the Gentiles” refers to Cornelius and his household. Peter points out that the outpouring of the gift of the Holy Spirit upon the Gentiles “as he did to us” (15:8) clearly demonstrates that God makes no distinction between Jews and Gentiles. God knew the hearts of the Gentiles and cleansed them without making a distinction between “us and them” because of their faith in Jesus Christ (15:9). Thus, according to Peter, imposing circumcision and the observance of the Mosaic law upon the Gentiles is unnecessary and in fact, would be putting God to the test. Peter concludes his speech by stating that salvation for both Jews and Gentiles comes through grace and faith and not through the law (15:11). Peter’s speech had a powerful effect on his listeners, and it changed the atmosphere of the assembly. Everyone grew silent and seemed willing to listen to Paul and Barnabas, who recounted “all the signs and wonders that God had done through them among the Gentiles” (15:12). It is noteworthy that Paul’s role in Acts 15 is kept to a minimum. The person with sufficient influence and authority to settle the matter was neither Peter nor Paul, but James.

James begins his speech (15:13-21) summarizing what Peter had said (15:14). The reference to Peter’s speech indicates his support and approval of Peter’s position and provides new insight into the conversion of Cornelius. James confirms that Peter’s Gentile mission is God’s initiative and affirms that circumcision is not necessary for Gentile believers. The descent of the Holy Spirit upon Cornelius and his household is irrefutable (15:28). Thus, there is no longer a distinction between Jews and Gentiles. To back up this new development James appeals to Scripture, citing Amos 9:11-12 in Acts 15:16-18. The predicted rebuilding of the promised Davidic house has been restored and the messianic age has arrived because “all the Gentiles” are seeking the Lord and invoking his name. Thus, Gentiles who are turning to God in faith should no longer be bothered with the burden of becoming Jewish proselytes. James settles the matter by proposing that a letter be written requiring several things from the Gentiles, namely, “to avoid pollution from idols, unlawful marriage, the meat of strangled animals, and blood” (15:20).

The four prohibitions (15:20,29) come from Leviticus 17–18 and are binding on the foreigners who sojourn in the land of Israel. Many interpretations have been suggested concerning the rationale for these requirements but the explanation that makes most sense is that Gentile Christians, living in the context of temples laden with pagan idols and cultic practices, completely renounce participation in pagan idols and being attentive to Jewish dietary laws would help facilitate Jewish and Gentile table fellowship, which is an important aspect when engaging in intercultural mission.

In agreement with the entire church, the apostles and elders selected a delegation to send to Antioch to deliver the news of the decision of the council in the form of a letter (15:23-29). The embedded apostolic letter contains all the typical elements of an ancient epistle, which was a common form of communication in the ancient world. The letter has an opening greeting that identifies the senders and the recipients (15:23). The body of the letter (15:24-29a) gives a brief historical background of the situation, introduces the envoy, and states the four requirements. The letter ends with an exhortation “to do well” and a closing greeting expressed in one simple word, “Farewell” (15:29b).

Delivering the Letter and Its Aftermath (15:30-35)

Barnabas and Paul returned to Antioch together with two representatives, Judas Barsabbas and Silas. Upon hearing the news that the Gentile believers neither have to be circumcised nor become Jewish proselytes, the entire community rejoiced at the encouragement. The two envoys from Jerusalem effectively carried out their duties by strengthening the community with many words of encouragement and after some time, they returned to Jerusalem taking with them “a blessing of peace” (15:33), which indicates a restoration of harmony and unity among the believers. The outcome allowed Paul and Barnabas to continue to teach and proclaim the word of the Lord with many others in Antioch.

There is much scholarly discussion about what actually took place at the Jerusalem council. While Luke’s account in Acts 15 seems to correspond with Paul’s account in Galatians 2:1-10, careful readers will notice that these two versions have various differences and even contain contradictory details. According to Luke, Paul visited Jerusalem thrice (Acts 9:26-29; 11:27-30; 15:1-5); Paul was sent by church in Antioch (15:2-3); the meeting was public (15:12); and Paul did not seem to play a major role in the debate. However, according to Paul in his letter to the Galatians, he visited Jerusalem only twice before the council; and he went there because of a revelation (Gal 2:2); and for Paul the gathering was a private one (Gal 2:2); and in Galatians, Paul was a major player in the discussion and decision making. What is most strikingly different is that in Acts, there was a decree issued concerning the observance of dietary laws and some other moral restrictions; however, according to Paul, there was no such decree. Due to the differences in the two accounts, some scholars suggest that there might have been two separate meetings that took place. One must have dealt with the issue of circumcision where Paul was present and contributed much; and the other meeting occurred to deal with the issue of dietary laws where Paul was absent. Thus, some distinguish these two events by calling the first “the Jerusalem Council” and the latter “the Jerusalem Decree.”

MISSION OF PAUL TO THE ENDS OF THE EARTH (15:36 – 28:31)

The Second Missionary Journey: From Antioch through Asia Minor to Greece and return (15:36 – 18:22)

The second half of Acts now becomes almost exclusively the story of Paul. After the Jerusalem Council, Paul decided to embark on a new missionary venture, taking with him Silas and eventually being joined by Timothy and Luke. The initial aim was to revisit and strengthen the churches that were established on the first journey and to deliver the decisions reached by the Apostolic Council. However, under the guidance of the Holy Spirit, Paul and his companions were prevented from preaching in certain regions and during this journey Paul crossed over into Europe for the first time by special divine guidance. Nonetheless, the journey and mission were not without difficulty since there was much opposition from unbelieving Jews as well as Gentiles. But the gospel continued to make headway among diverse groups of people. This arduous expedition lasted for about two and a half years (49-52 CE). Probably during this period Paul wrote most of his preserved undisputed letters.

Mission in Lycaonia and Asia Minor (15:36 – 16:10)

The new missionary journey kicked off to a rocky start. Paul and Barnabas had a sharp disagreement about whether to allow John Mark to come along on this journey. The dispute was so severe that Paul and Barnabas had to part company. Barnabas took Mark and sailed for Cyprus. References from several Pauline letters (Phlm 24; Col 4:10; 2 Tim 4:11; 1 Cor 9:6) indicate that Paul and Mark (also Barnabas) were later reconciled. Paul and Silas travelled through Syria and Cilicia, visiting, and strengthening the churches and the reached Derbe and Lystra located in southern Galatia. Here, Paul met Timothy (Timotheos) whose name means “one who honours God.”

Timothy is described as a “disciple” who had a good reputation among the believers in Lystra and Iconium (16:2). Timothy was a child of mixed marriage and recognizing his great potential, Paul recruited him as a co-worker and circumcised him “on account of the Jews” (16:3). Paul’s initiative to circumcise Timothy has stirred much debate considering Paul’s position at the Jerusalem Council (15:1-2) and how he consistently maintained that Gentile believers did not need to be circumcised in order to have full salvation (1 Cor 7:18-19; Gal 2:1-5; 5:1-12). Several plausible explanations have been proposed: (a) Since Timothy was Jewish and not yet circumcised, Paul might have thought that for the benefit of mission among the Jews, it would be less problematic if Timothy were circumcised. (b) Luke wants to show that Paul was not being disloyal or insensitive to the Judaic laws and customs. (c) Church’s mission to the Gentiles does not require that Jews have to forego their Jewishness and that the church had not completely given up on them. Paul’s circumcision of Timothy later helps Paul defends against the claim made by Paul’s opponents that he taught Jews not “to circumcise their children or to

observe their customary practices” (21:21). As for Timothy, he proved to be an extraordinary associate of Paul (Acts 17:14-15; 18:5; 19:22; 20:4) who co-authored several Pauline letters (1

Thess. 1:1; 2 Cor 1:1; Phil 1:1; Phlm 1) and was the recipient of two Pastoral Letters (1 & 2 Tim).

Guided by the “Spirit of Jesus,” Paul and his companions passed through Mysia and went down to Troas, a seaport city located on the ancient site of Troy and on the Aegean coast. There, Paul received a vision of a man urging him to come to Macedonia to help the people there (16:9). Their immediate departure for Macedonia indicates that the missionaries interpreted the vision as a clear sign of God’s providential guidance. Another notable feature in 16:10 is the usage of the first-person plural narration (“we”). These “we Passages” are found in four sections of Acts (16:10-17; 20:5-15; 21:1-18; 27:1–28:16). There is no scholarly consensus on the interpretation of these “we Passages” and three interpretations are proposed: (1) the writer was a genuine historical eyewitness; (2) the writer used someone else’s travel log and edited them for his traveling narratives; and (3) the author invented these accounts as a stylistic device to give more credibility to the narrative.

Mission to Philippi (16:11-40)

Luke now recounts the story of Paul’s first visit to Philippi, a Roman colony. It was named after King Philip II, the father of Alexander the Great who had captured it in 356 BCE and renamed it after himself. Philippi is known as the gateway to Macedonia and here Paul had established a Christian community, to which he eventually sent his Letter to the Philippians.

The evangelizing in Philippi shows us some of the best and the worst of a mission among Gentiles. The generous openness and support of Lydia, a Gentile devotee of Jewish worship, is a model for the Christian household. She is described as gracious, hospitable, and extremely generous. Her house became a house church, a place of refuge, and a frequent rest stop for traveling missionaries (16:15,40). On the other hand, the legal and financial problems presented by the girl who had a spirit of divination reminds us that Paul was dealing with an alien, superstitious world. Paul himself attests that he was imprisoned and beaten with rods (2 Cor 11:23-25). The miraculous opening of the prison echoes scenes of the apostles and of Peter’s miraculous release from prison (5:19-20; 12:6-11) and shows that God is with his emissary to the Gentiles. The story of deliverance begins with an earthquake, which in the biblical and Hellenistic tradition signifies the presence of divine power. The complexity of Paul’s trial because he is a Roman citizen illustrates how the early Christian, in order to survive, had to use every available means, including Roman law. The “we” for of narrative ceases as Paul leaves Philippi, and so it implies that Luke remained in Philippi. The second “We Passage” will resume at Philippi (20:5-15), approximately seven years later (56-57 CE) during Paul’s third missionary journey.

Mission in Thessalonica and Beroea (17:1-15)

Thessalonica was a thriving commercial centre and capital of the province of Macedonia. It was named after the wife of a Macedonian leader, Thessalonike, who was the daughter of King Philip II. Here, Paul ran into the same kind of Jewish opposition (against Paul’s proclamation that Jesus is the promised Messiah) that marred his mission in Asia Minor before the Jerusalem council. The list of charges against Paul and Silas in 16:6-7 resembles the list of charges against Jesus before Pilate in Luke 23:2. Fearful of greater disorder and violence, the Thessalonian officials took bail from Jason and other believers probably with the assurance that Paul and Silas would leave immediately and with good behaviour. Luke gives no explanation of what exactly the bail was. Whatever it was, the charges were dismissed, and the missionaries were able to slip away in the night.

Forced by Jewish opposition Paul goes to Beroea (17:10-14) where Luke tells us that the Jews were nobler and less contentious. They listened to Paul’s deliberations from the Scriptures and therefore received the message with all eagerness. Both Jews and Gentiles, including prominent Greek women and men, came to believe in Jesus. But, the Jews from Thessalonica follow, and so Paul pushes on to Athens (17:15-34). Silas and Timothy, however, were able to stay behind in Beroea. Some of the believers escorted Paul by ship to Athens and having reached Athens safely, Paul sent the Christians back with the instruction that Silas and Timothy “join him as soon as possible” (17:15). Silas and Timothy will join company with Paul not in Athens but later in Corinth (18:5).

Mission in Athens (17:16-34)

Athens was the centre of Greek culture and philosophy. It is the city that produced many great philosophers like Socrates and Plato. Although Athens was well past its golden age by the time Paul arrived, it was still considered university city, and intellectuals from all over the Roman Empire were drawn to it. One customary activity of the Athenian intellectuals was to gather in public squares (stoa) and marketplaces (agora) to debate any new theory or idea that surfaced. Athens was also a religious centre for worship with temples and shrines everywhere in the city. There was even a shrine dedicated to an “unknown god” (17:23).

In Athens, Paul began teaching in the synagogue and the marketplaces about Jesus as the Christ. Perhaps Paul’s preaching about Jesus and the resurrection (anastasis) had led some to assume that Paul wanted to introduce new deities. Thus, Paul was summoned before the Council of Areopagus which in ancient Athens functioned as the high court of appeal for civil (also possibly criminal) cases.

Paul seemed to have done his homework well. He walked around observing everything carefully (17:23). His speech indicates that he understood the local philosophies and was quite knowledgeable about the Greek and Roman gods. He knew their way of thinking and living. He quoted Greek poets and philosophers. He begins his speech with their own respect for an

“unknown God” and build on it, to bring them to a proper understanding of the living God, who is the creator of the world, is close to human beings, and even related to them. This God, he maintains, will judge human beings, and then he introduces a reference to the risen Christ, through whom God will carry out this judgement.

Paul employs different rhetorical techniques in his speech which can be divided into four parts: (a) Exordium or Introduction (vv. 22-23a): It is designed to establish rapport and credibility with his listeners. He compliments them for being “extremely religious in every way.” After gaining their attention, Paul proceeds to the next stage. (b) Propositio or Proposition (v 23b): Paul declares his intended goal of the speech to make known the Unknown God. (c) Probatio or Proof (vv. 24-29): Paul quotes several familiar Greek poets and philosophers to strengthen the argument that this Unknown God is none other than the Creator God which he came to proclaim. Paul tries to convince the Athenians that images fashioned by humans are mere distortions of the image of the one true God. He points out that their time of “groping search” or fumbling in the darkness is over. Paul then concludes with (d) Peroratio or Exhortation (vv.30-31): Paul attempts to persuade his listeners to repent and to belief in the one true God. Finally, Paul refers to the controversial issue of “the resurrection” which he delayed until now.

Paul’s ultimate purpose of the speech was to change his audience’s beliefs and behaviour. He did it with great sensitivity and rhetorical skill. He used whatever persuasive tools he had acquired to engage the Athenians’ worldview and philosophy. This sermon of Paul is an outstanding example of cross-cultural missionary preaching. While showing cultural sensitivity to the Greeks, Paul proclaimed the Christian message with integrity and boldness. However, at the mention of the resurrection of the dead, some sneered while others walked away in disbelief. Nevertheless, Paul speech was not completely unsuccessful. First, the Athenians did not oppose Paul’s arguments, and some considered hearing him again. Second few people like Dionysius, Damaris and others did believe in Paul. Some lack of success is again part of what Paul has to suffer on behalf of Jesus’ name.

Mission in Corinth (18:1-17)

Paul’s next stop was Corinth, the capital of the Roman province of Achaia and an important economic and administrative centre of the region. In Paul’s day, Corinth had become a major crossroad for travellers and merchants. As a major urban centre, the city had many temples honouring multiple deities and promoting imperial cuts and it offered various forms of entertainment. Here Paul lived and worked for at least eighteen months (18:11) and established one of the most important Christian communities.

When Paul comes to Corinth, he resides with Aquila and Priscilla, who became his trusted friends and collaborators. Luke gives us three separate descriptions of Priscilla and Aquila: (a) 18:1-3 tells that they are married couple who were expelled from Rome. They immigrated to Corinth where Paul met them and resided in their home. The three worked together as tentmakers

or as leatherworkers. Luke’s brief description seems to indicate that they had been Christ believers before they left Rome. (b) 18: 18-19 tells us that they were missionary companions of Paul and cofounders of the church in Ephesus. This indicates that they sacrificed everything they had built up in Corinth to accompany Paul across the Aegean Sea to Ephesus. (c) 18:26-27 credits Priscilla and Aquila as the catechizers who taught the great preacher Apollos, a Jewish native of Alexandria who was well versed in the Scriptures (18:24). The teaching by Priscilla and Aquila was so effective that after only a brief time Apollos wished to move on to Achaia, perhaps to bring the good news there also.

We know a lot more about this migrant missionary couple since they also appear in three Pauline letters (1 Cor 16:19; Rom 16:3-5; 2 Tim 4:19). Priscilla as Luke consistently calls her, is always referred to by Paul by her formal name, Prisca. The couple were exemplary immigrant missionaries who brought the faith with them wherever they went.

In Corinth, Paul devoted himself to preaching to Jews and Greeks in the synagogue that Jesus was the Christ (18:4). When Silas and Timothy arrived in Corinth, Paul’s preaching ministry became more intense. This eventually led to severe opposition from the Jews which led Paul to protest and to declare that “from now on” he will go to the Gentiles (18:6). Two notable converts are named – Titius Justus who was a God fearer living next to the synagogue, and Crispus who was president of the synagogue and baptized by Paul (1 Cor 1:14). Luke narrates that the Jews were united in opposing Paul (18:12), and so they brought him before Gallio who declared that Paul had not committed any serious crime. The whole incident of Paul before Gallio (18:12-17) must have occurred sometime in 51 CE since Paul’s eighteen month stay in Corinth was likely from 50 to 52 Ce and this date is quite reliably accurate since it coincides with Gallio’s time as Proconsul of Achaia from 51 CE to 52 CE.

While ministering in Corinth, possibly soon after Silas and Timothy arrived with the latest news from Boroea, Paul wrote First Thessalonians.

Paul Returns to Antioch (18:18-22)

This scene relates Paul’s return to Antioch. The travel on sea and land lasts for several months. At the port at Cenchreae, Paul cut his hair because of a vow that he had taken. He then embarks for Ephesus, accompanied by Priscilla and Aquila. After a brief stopover in Ephesus, Paul continues his journey alone to Jerusalem and then to Antioch in Syria.

The Third Missionary Journey: From Antioch to Ephesus and Greece, and return to Caesarea (18:23 – 21:14)

After spending some time in Antioch, Paul quickly embarked on a new missionary outreach. The main goal of this journey was to revisit the previously established churches and strengthen all the believers, but when Paul reached Ephesus, he settled there. Paul’s mission in Ephesus

(19:1-40) dominates the third missionary journey. This section culminates with an emotional farewell speech to the Ephesian elders at Miletus (20:17-38), which effectively summarizes Paul’s mission in Ephesus as well as the entire three missionary journeys. While at Ephesus, Paul is believed to have written letters to the Corinthians [1 Cor & a lost letter (1 Cor 5:9)] and during this journey, Paul also wrote his letters to Galatians, to Philippians and to the Romans.

Mission in Ephesus (18:23 – 19:40)

Ephesus was the capital of western Asia Minor and was ranked second in importance and size only to Rome. With a very important sea harbour and as a major centre of commerce, Ephesus enjoyed rapid growth and great prosperity. Additionally, Ephesus was the home of the famous Greek goddess Artemis and was worshipped in the great Temple of Artemis.

To set the stage for Paul’s arrival, Luke first recounts the evangelistic work of Apollos in Ephesus (18:24-28). While Apollos was in Corinth, Paul arrives in Ephesus. He is amazed to encounter disciples who had not even heard of the Holy Spirit, for they had only received the John’s baptism of repentance of sins to be ready to welcome the Messiah. Thus, Paul lays his hands on them, and they receive the Holy Spirit, which was confirmed by the gifts of glossolalia and prophesy (19:6; cf. 2:4,17; 10:44-48). After three months of preaching and teaching about the “kingdom of God” Paul encounters stiff resistance from the Jews which forces him to move his mission from the synagogue to the lecture hall of Tyrannus. For two years Paul preaches the gospel unhindered and was quite successful among both “Jews and Greeks alike” (19:10).

God manifests extraordinary power through Paul such that even his face cloths/handkerchiefs or apron had miraculous powers to cure the sick and those who were possessed by evil spirits (19:12). Witnessing these miraculous phenomena, the seven sons of Sceva, who were itinerant Jewish exorcists try to imitate Paul but the evil spirit attacks and overpowers them forcing them to flee. Luke narrates the dramatic account concerning the seven sons of Sceva (19:13-20) with succinctness and vividness to deal with the highly complex and controversial issue of miracle versus magic in early Christianity. The efforts and knowledge of exorcists were futile and had disastrous results because the evil spirit did not acknowledge their authority. The Ephesians saw the defeat of the seven exorcists as a defeat of magic and thus they repented of their magical practices and burned their magical books. Fifty thousand silver coins (19:19) have enormous value, equalling approximately 50,000 days wage for an ordinary person. Since magic was an aspect of everyday life in antiquity, Luke recognizes that magic can pose a real and appealing threat to the early Christians. Luke takes great pains to directly address the issue of magic in early Christianity (8:9-25; 13:6-12; 14:8-20; 16:16-21; 19:13-20). Luke’s anti-magic apologetic strategy is to demonstrate that magic should have no place in Christianity.

The next incident recounts the riot of the silversmiths led by Demetrius (19:23-40). Demetrius accuses Paul of saying that “gods made with hands are not gods” (19:26), which accurately reflects what Paul had been teaching (14:15-17; 17:24-29). Demetrius’ speech claiming that Paul’s preaching of Christian message was detrimental to their business, causes

fear and anger among the guild of silversmiths. This leads them to attack two of Paul’s companions. The riot is finally subdued by the town clerk who makes it clear that such riotous conduct of the craftsmen might bring them into conflict with the duly constituted city authorities.

Revisiting the Churches in Macedonia, Achaia, and Asia (20:1-16)

The mission in Ephesus ends with the departure of Paul for Macedonia. Luke already foretold Paul’s decision to make this journey in 19:21-22. The verb dei (“it is necessary”) implies that the decision to visit Rome was guided by the Holy Spirit and conforms fully to God’s will. Having visited the believers in Macedonia, Paul then travelled to Greece and stayed in Corinth for three months (20:3). From Greece Paul returns to Macedonia and then sails for Troas. The list of seven travelling companions (20:4) likely includes the representatives of the churches that contributed to the relief project. It is at Troas that Luke reappears on the scene; thus, the second “We Passage” begins (20:5-15). The “breaking of bread” points to a eucharistic celebration (20:7; cf. 2:42,46), and the “first day of the week” probably refers to a Sunday, which starts at sundown on Saturday. Paul’s gesture of healing resembles that of Elijah stretching himself upon the dead boy whom he resuscitated (1 Kgs 17:21). Luke shows that Paul too has the ability to restore a dead person to life, just like the apostle Peter (9:36-41). Vv. 14-16 describe the journey from Assos to Miletus which takes several days by boat to sail.

Paul’s Farewell Speech at Miletus (20:17-38)

This speech of Paul before the Ephesian elders is the only one addressed exclusively to a Christian audience. These elders are the current leaders of one of the main churches founded by Paul. Paul’s farewell speech echoes his past ministry among the Ephesians (20:18b-21), examines his present situation (20:22-24), and predicts his future trials (20:25-31). The structure of the speech is marked by the three “and now” phrases (20:22,25,32) and concludes with a commendation and blessing (20:32-35).

Several key features are worth highlighting about the character of Paul as a model church leader. Paul is a dedicated missionary who works very hard (“night and day”) to support himself so as not to burden others for the sake of the gospel. He is a humble servant who refuses to claim anything for himself. He is not afraid to show love and affection for those to whom he ministers. He demonstrates remarkable consistency in words and actions and has tremendous patience in hardship. He conveys his message through lived examples and thus has the integrity to ask others to imitate him. He exercises authority in a spirit of generosity and sacrificial service that is unpretentious. Paul’s farewell address teaches not only the elders at Ephesus but also all church ministers the typical qualities of leadership. The sermon ends with a saying of Jesus, “it is more blessed to give than to receive” (20:35), a beatitude not found anywhere in the Gospels.

Paul’s journey to Jerusalem (21:1-14)

The return journey to Palestine brings Paul to Tyre (21:1-6) and another dramatic farewell, and then on to Caesarea (21:7-14). There at the home of Philip the Hellenist and his four daughter-prophets, the prophet Agabus comes and by symbolism forewarns Paul of imprisonment. Thus, Paul’s road to Jerusalem and impending suffering echoes Jesus’ journey to Jerusalem where he would be seized and put to death (Luke 9:51; 13:33).

Arrest in Jerusalem; Imprisonment and Trials in Caesarea (21:15 – 26:32)

Arrest in Jerusalem (21:15 – 23:35)

Paul’s missionary journey comes to an end as he makes his way towards Jerusalem. This major section of Acts is dominated by the events and circumstances surrounding Paul’s arrest and trials in Jerusalem (21:15–23:35) and in Caesarea (24:1–26:32). The most notable feature in this section is Paul’s four defense speeches: the first before the Jewish crowd in the Jerusalem temple (22:1-21) and the next three in Caesarea before Felix (24:10-21), Festus (25:8-11), and Agrippa (26:2-23). The amount of space devoted to Paul’s arrest and trials in which Paul is allowed to speak for himself demonstrates Luke’s main apologetic motif to defend Paul’s innocence. At the same time Luke provides the platform for Paul to continue to give direct testimony to Jesus as the risen Christ and to defend the legitimacy of “the Way.”

Paul’s Meeting with James and Jerusalem Elders (21:15-25)

Climax is reached when Paul goes up to Jerusalem (21:15-17), where the “we” form of description comes to an end (21:18), not to be resumed until six chapters and two years later. Paul is received by James and the elders and Paul reports to them his success among the Gentiles (21:19). After hearing Paul’s report, James and the elders “praised God” (21:20), which is an acknowledgement of God’s blessing. In the address that follows (21:20-25), James (the implied spokesperson) cites the rumours that Paul was teaching believers to abandon their Jewish ways (21:21). Readers know that these charges are groundless since Paul had Timothy circumcised (16:3) and had been quite faithful to his own Jewish traditions (18:18; 20:6,16). To refute these accusations, James suggests that Paul make a public demonstration to prove that he is a law- observant Jew (21:24). This requires Paul (along with four other men) participate in a purification ritual and pay the necessary expenses for the sacrifices at the end of the Nazirite vows (Num. 6:9-10,13-15,21). The last part of the speech (21:25) reiterates the decisions that the apostles and elders had previously agreed upon concerning the stipulations for the Gentile believers (15:28-29).

Paul’s Arrest (21:26-40)

Paul act on James’ suggestion to begin the process of purification that would take seven days (21:26-27). Just before the purification ritual was completed, Jews from Asia recognized Paul in the temple and instigated the crowd to arrest him. They accused Paul of teaching against the Jewish people, against the law, and against the temple. The charge of defiling the sacred temple is purely speculative. They seized Paul and dragged him out of the temple in order to kill him. The “shutting of the gates” (21:30) could symbolically represent the ultimate rejection of the Christian message. The Temple henceforth would have no meaning for the Christian church. The whole city was now in chaos and the Roman soldiers had to rush to the scene to restore order and prevent the unruly crowd from killing Paul. Through this Luke makes a point that Paul is not the one who causes political uprising or jeopardizes the law and order of the Roman world. Sensing that Paul’s life is in danger, soldiers carry Paul away into the barracks of the fortress. Meanwhile, the crowd kept following and shouting, “Take him away!” The scene and shouts echo Jesus’ trial before Pilate (Luke 23:18). Paul politely asks the commander for permission to address the crowd that has come together.

Paul’s Defense Speech before the Jerusalem Crowd (22:1-21)

This is the first of several “defense” speeches of Paul. To show that he is not guilty of defiling the temple or disparaging Jewish traditions, Paul gives a biographical sketch of his origins and training. As a diaspora Jew who was zealous for God, Paul moved to Jerusalem to study under Gamaliel, the leading rabbi of the time (22:3; cf. 5:34). His zeal for God and the law led him to become an ardent persecutor of the followers of Jesus. In 22:6-21 Paul tells his “conversion” experience on the way to Damascus. Many of the details are similar to the narrator’s account in 9:3-19. Several minor differences or added details are stylistic emphases and can be explained by the fact that this account is told by the character in the story (Paul) rather than by the main narrator (Luke). Paul adds a post-conversion vision that he had during a visit to the temple in Jerusalem (22:17-21), which ends with Lord directing Paul to go to the Gentiles (22:21) and it corresponds to Paul’s commission in Acts 9:15 and in Galatians 1:16. Paul’s retelling of his conversion and commission echoes biblical call stories (Isa 6; Jer. 1) and includes details that give greater proofs of his faithfulness to God and piety towards Judaism.

Paul’s Interrogations in Jerusalem (22:22–23:11)

The mention of “Gentiles” evokes a reaction from the crowd, which is an urge to do away with Paul. The commander of the Romans, who have been keeping watch over the episode, decides to intervene. He orders Paul to be arrested and determines to interrogate him about the cause of the uprising, but under a lash. But when Paul tells a centurion standing by that he is a Roman citizen, and when that is reported to the tribune, he is released. The tribune’s citizenship

was purchased by money, which meant he was lower in status than Paul, who had acquired his from birth. The next day the tribune ordered the chief priests and the entire Sanhedrin to assemble to determine the charges brought against Paul by the Jews (23:1-11). Paul stood before the Sanhedrin with courage. He begins by asserting that he is a law-abiding Jew who has lived “with a clear conscience” (23:1). The command to strike Paul on the mouth is to show that he is a liar and also to silence him. Paul, who refused to be intimidated, called the high priest a “whitewashed wall,” which is a vivid metaphor for hypocrisy, possibly alluding to Ezekiel’s reference concerning false prophets (Ezek. 13:10-15). Recognizing that there were both Sadducees and Pharisees present, Paul acknowledges that he is a Pharisee which was a tactical move aimed to divide the assembly. In fact, the assembly became so divided and intense that the tribune must, once again, intervene to save Paul from being torn into pieces. That night in the barracks and in protective custody, Paul receives a vision of the Lord standing at his side encouraging him and assuring him that he will be kept safe and that he must also bear witness in Rome (23:11).

The Conspiracy against Paul and His Transfer to Caesarea (23:12-35)

While the Lord promises to protect Paul, some Jews conspire to assassinate him. Luke discloses their scheme in vivid detail (23:12-15). Verses 16-22 relate how the plot is discovered. Paul’s nephew, the son of his sister, somehow heard about the ambush and he visits Paul at the fortress to inform him of the scheme. In verses 20-21 Paul’s nephew repeats the details of the conspiracy to the tribune. The tribune then immediately plans to transfer Paul safely to Felix who is the governor residing in Caesarea (23:23-35). The tribune organizes an impressive expedition which conveys the severity of the threat. Verses 24-30 contain the content of the letter with a standard epistolary prescript. The letter contains a standard salutario, chairein (Hail! / Salute! / Greetings!) the rest of the letter gives a concise report from the perspective of the tribune on the events that led to the Jewish conspiracy against Paul and the reasons for transferring him to Caesarea (23:27-30). The tribune recommends that Paul’s case be further investigated and that the accusers must appear before the governor to state their allegations. Paul is safely transferred to Caesarea via Antipatris. Since Paul’s citizenship falls under Felix’s jurisdiction, Felix agrees to hear Paul’s case, but in the meantime, he places Paul under guard in “Herod’s praetorium” (official residence).

Paul’s Trial and Imprisonment in Caesarea (24:1–26:32)

This long episode is comprised of four speeches: the first one by Tertullus, who is the lawyer representing the Jerusalem plaintiffs (24:2-8) and the other three by Paul, the defendant (24:10- 21; 25:8-11; 26:2-23). Paul will speak before two Roman governors (Felix and Festus) and a Jewish king (Agrippa II), and two of three political authorities will declare that Paul is innocent. Nevertheless, Paul is kept in custody for two years.

The Trial before Felix (24:1-27)

This incident may be divided into three units: the prosecution (vv.1-9), the defense (vv. 10-21)   and the verdict (vv.22-27). This is the only place in Acts where speeches take the form of a debate. The Jewish authorities have brought formal charges against Paul before Felix in Caesarea. The presence of the high priest Ananias demonstrates the gravity of this case. Tertullus’ speech is polished and brief. He charges Paul with two offenses: being a political agitator (24:5) and profaning the sacred temple (24:6). He calls Paul a disease of epidemic proportions, such as plague or pestilence and a “ringleader” of the sect of the Nazarenes. The term “sect” has a derogatory sense here, and Luke never uses it to designate the followers of Jesus. Paul will correctly identify the Jesus movement as “The Way” (24:14).

Paul gives his self defense speech (24:10-21) that is structured according to Roman court proceedings. In exordium (introduction, v.10), Paul acknowledges Felix as a “judge.” In the narratio (a summary of the facts of the case, v. 11), Paul describes the purpose of his visit to Jerusalem. In probatio (Proofs vv. 12-13) Paul gives three proofs for his innocence: that he was not involved in any disputes with anyone, that he did not organize a crowd, and that the charges cannot be proven. In the refutatio (refutation vv. 14-18) Paul contests the charges of being a “ringleader” of the Nazarenes and of having desecrated the temple. Paul concludes his speech (peroratio, vv.19-21) by stating that the charges brought against him are ridiculous. Paul insists that the reason for his incarceration and trial involves only a Jewish religious matter that has no political basis.

Interestingly we are told that Felix knew about the Way (24:22). Despite knowing that Paul and the Christians are not rabble-rousers creating riots and posing political threats to Rome, Felix keeps Paul in prison for two years (24:27) because he wants to extort a bribe (24:26) and to grant a “favour” to the Jews (24:27). According to Josephus, Felix was so captivated by the beauty of Drusilla (daughter of Herod Agrippa I of Acts 12; and sister of Bernice and King Herod Agrippa II of 25:13), that he had arranged for a magician to persuade her to leave her husband and marry him. It is no wonder Felix became frightened (24:25) when Paul spoke about “righteousness,” “self-control,” and “coming judgment.”

The Trial before Festus (25:1-12)

Sometime in 59 CE, Felix was called to Rome because of corruption and failure to deal with the social unrest and was replaced by Porcius Festus as the new procurator of Judea. Although two years had passed, the Jewish authorities have not forgotten the case of Paul. The Jewish leaders request a “favour” from the new governor to transfer Paul’s case from Caesarea to Jerusalem. The real reason is to ambush and kill Paul, as planned two years earlier (23:12-25). However, Festus refuses to grant their request. Verses 6-12 give a succinct report of Paul’s trial in Caesarea. The accusers from Jerusalem brought “many serious charges” against Paul that they

could not prove (25:7). Paul asserts that he is innocent of all three charges: against the law of the Jews, against the temple, and against the emperor (25:8). The third charge appears to be a new charge. Paul’s “self-defence” is described in one single verse (24:8). Paul’s status as a Roman citizen allows him to make this appeal to be tried before the tribunal of Caesar. After consulting with his council, Festus grants Paul’s request declaring, “You have appealed to Caesar. To Caesar you will go” (25:12). This is another major turning point of the narrative, which has been anticipated by Paul, to bear witness to Christ even in Rome (19:21; 23:11).

The Hearing before King Agrippa (25:13 – 26:32)

Granting Paul’s petition to be tried in Rome proves to be a good solution for Festus to get himself off the hook, especially with the Jewish authorities. But he needs to explain the reasons for granting such a request to the emperor. The courtesy visit of King Agrippa and Bernice (25:13) provides Festus with the perfect occasion to obtain further information to be used in the letter (see 25:26). So, Festus summarizes the case of Paul to King Agrippa in the form of a speech (25:14-22). Festus was able to identify the heart of the issue – a religious disagreement between Paul and the Jewish leaders concerning the resurrection of Jesus (see 25:19). Agrippa became intrigued and wished to hear Paul (25:22).

The scene of Paul’s hearing in the audience hall (25:23–26:32) in front of a king, a governor, military commanders, and the most prominent dignitaries is impressive, echoing and fulfilling the prophecy of Jesus who warned about future persecution and being led before kings and governors because of him (Luke 21:12). Festus declares Paul’s innocence and that he has found nothing that deserves a death sentence (25:24-25). But because he has granted Paul’s appeal to the emperor, Festus now solicits advice to obtain concrete charges to be included in the letter that he must write to the emperor (25:26-27).

Acts 26:2-23 is Paul’s fourth self-defence speech in Acts (his eighth of ten speeches so far). Paul demonstrates exceptional rhetorical skills as a speaker. His hand gesture (26:1), language and procedure conform to the standards of Greek rhetoric. Paul first acknowledges the presence of King Agrippa and considers himself “fortunate” to be able to defend himself before the King whom he regards as an expert in Jewish affairs. The structure of Paul’s speech can be seen thus:

Introduction (captatio benevolentiae – to win the support of the hearer):           26:2-3

I.  Paul’s Jewish, Pharisaic life and belief:                                                            26:4-8

II.     Paul’s persecution of Christians and conversion:                                           26:9-18

III.   Paul’s testimony about the Suffering Messiah:                                              26:19-23

With Festus’ interruption (26:24), the speech comes to an end. What follows is the reaction of Festus and Agrippa as well as Paul’s response to both (26:24-32). The episode ends dramatically with a double declaration of Paul’s innocence by all the participants at the hearing (26:31) and specifically by King Agrippa (26:32). This is the third time that Paul is pronounced innocent in

Acts (23:29; 25:25; 26:31-32). Jesus was also declared innocent three times by Pontius Pilate (Luke 23:4, 14, 22).

This speech has been described as “the climax of all of Paul’s speeches.” In this discourse the emphasis is on Paul the “prophet,” contrast to Chapter 9 (Paul the chosen instrument) and Chapter 22 (Paul the witness). This emphasis is brought out by the allusions to OT prophets in 26:16-18 (to Ezek. 2:1-6; Jer. 1:8; Isa 35:5; 42:7; 61:1) and by reference to Moses and the prophets in support of his message (26:22). Though the discourse purports to be a defense of Paul, its hidden agenda is a defense of Christianity, which is now set out in its relation to Judaism. It contains features that fit as a missionary sermon (vv. 6-8, 23) that gives testimony to Jesus as the risen Christ – the Christological climax of the whole of Acts.

Journey to Rome as a Prisoner (27:1 – 28:16)

After more than two years in captivity (24:27), Paul is finally transferred from Caesarea to Rome to stand trial before Caesar. His arrival in Rome fulfils Jesus’ commission to be his witnesses from “Jerusalem…to the ends of the earth” (1:8). Luke devotes much discourse-time to Paul’s journey to Rome – a long sea journey up the Syrian coast, over past Cyprus, along the southern coast of Asia Minor, across to the southern coast of Crete, and then amidst a great storm, to Malta, Sicily, and up the west coast of Italy, to a landing at Puteoli, near Naples. The entire sea voyage, told in the form of the first-person plural narration, is best explained as deriving from an eyewitness companion. Survival from storm and snakebite illustrates God’s care for Paul whose concern for companions on the ship and healings effected at Malta show that his missionary sense has not left him. Paul’s journey to Rome is divided into three segments: from Caesarea to Crete (27:1-12), the storm at sea and shipwreck (27:13-44), and from Malta to Rome (28:1-16).

From Caesarea to Crete (27:1-12)

This scene records the journey from Caesarea to Crete. Paul, the prisoner was accompanied by Aristarchus (see 19:29; 20:4; Col 4:10; Phlm 24) and Luke, the author of the “We Passages” (16:10-17; 20:5-15; 21:1-18; 27:1–28:16). They board a trading ship travelling between Adramyttium, in western Asia Minor, and Alexandria in Egypt. The ship made a stopover in Sidon, where Paul was treated “kindly” (27:3) by the centurion and was allowed to visit “friends” or probably fellow believers. As the journey progressed, it became increasingly more difficult due to unfriendly wind and they had to sail along the southern coast of Crete and with much effort arrived at Kaloi Limenes, often translated as “Fair Havens.” Luke notes that the time for sailing had now become dangerous because the “fast (27:9), which refers to the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:29-31), had already passed. Paul, who in his letter claimed to have been shipwrecked several times (2 Cor 11:25), offered a prudent admonition, strongly urging them not to venture out to sea (27:10). But Paul’s warning was not heeded.

The Storm at Sea and Shipwreck (27:13-44)

Shortly after leaving Fair Havens, a storm with hurricane-force winds blew down from the mountains and swept the ship away from the coast of Crete toward the island of Cauda. Various emergency measures were taken to protect and secure the vessel (27:16-17) but the strong winds and heavy waves proved too much for the crew. For two weeks (27:27) the ship and crew were tossed about at sea and were completely lost. The crew and passengers finally gave up all hope of “being saved” (27:20). Verses 21-26 are a hortatory speech by Paul aimed at helping and sustaining fellow travellers who are sailing with him. Paul assures them that everyone on board would be saved, but not the ship (27:22). Paul’s prediction will come to pass in vv. 41-44. Paul ended the speech with words of encouragement (27:25) and another prediction (27:26). Luke then narrates the events that led to the shipwreck, but the tragic scene ends on a positive note with everyone safely swimming or floating to shore, just as Paul had foretold.

This episode presents Paul the prisoner as the man full of faith who dominates his fearful situation. He counsels the ship’s crew, and the Romans with whom he is travelling. He assures them that all will be saved, even if the ship itself is wrecked. He counsels his fellow passengers to take food to sustain themselves. All this confidence comes from his faith in the God to whom he belongs and whom he serves (27:23).

From Malta to Rome (28:1-16)

The aftermath of the shipwreck is now recounted by Luke. The worst is over; the shipwrecked party finds shelter on the shore of Malta. They are welcomed by the natives and are said to be tremendously “kind” or “hospitable.” There they spend the winter. Paul survives the attack of a viper, and the natives think he is god. Luke relates another story (28:7-10) about the miraculous healing of the father of Publius, the chief official of the island. Other people on the island who had illnesses heard about Paul’s healing powers and also came to be cured. This incident echoes the curing of Peter’s mother-in-law (Luke 4:38-39). Paul is not a god, but the chosen instrument of the risen Lord and is bringing his testimony gradually to Rome, the end of the earth.

After wintering for three months on Malta, Paul and his companions take ship from Malta, stop at Syracuse in Sicily, and sail on to Rhegium and then to Puteoli, where Paul lodges with Christians for a week. Then they continue their journey. Having heard that Paul was approaching, representatives of the Christian community in Rome goes out to meet him on the Via Appia. Paul was grateful and was encouraged to see them. When Paul gets to Rome, he is allowed to take a lodging of his own, with a soldier to guard him (28:16). This episode ends where the “We Passage” also ends.

Paul in Rome (28:17-31)

Luke ends the second volume of his writing with a telling and climactic episode about Paul’s testimony in Rome, the capital of the civilized world of that time. He has depicted Paul journeying “to the end of the earth” (1:8), and also relates how he continues, even as a prisoner, to bear testimony to the Word of God in Rome. This final episode of Acts relates two incidents: Paul’s meeting with the Jewish leaders in Rome (28:17-29) and Paul’s bold proclamation of the kingdom of God (28:30-31).

Paul’s Encounter with the Jews (28:17-29)

Paul wastes no time in summoning the Jewish leaders in Rome for a meeting. Then he delivers two speeches. The first (vv.17-20) is a defense speech and Paul makes four points: (a) He has done nothing against his people or its ancestral customs; (b) the Romans wanted to set him free; (c) when Jerusalem authorities objected to his release, he appealed to Caesar; and (d) he is prisoner being tried for “the hope of Israel.” This speech is interrupted by a statement of the Jewish leaders about having heard nothing against him and that they were open to hear Paul’s view on the matter. On their second meeting, Paul spends the entire day testifying to “the kingdom of God.” Paul tries to convince the Jewish leaders that Jesus is the promised Messiah spoken of by Moses and foretold by the prophets. This resembles Paul’s speech to diaspora Jews in Pisidian Antioch (13:16-41). A mixed reception by Paul’s audience is a familiar pattern in Acts (13:42-43; 14:1-2; 17:1-5; 19:8-10).

The second speech (vv. 25c-28) is an accusation and here Paul makes three points: (a) Paul again bears testimony to the Jews of Rome; (b) he analyses the Jewish rejection of the gospel; and (c) he shows how the message of God’s salvation is consequently being sent to Gentiles. In this speech, Paul makes use of the OT and quotes Isa 6:9-10. Paul’s declaration seems harsh because it reflects the reality of Luke’s time that Israel, as a whole, has not been receptive to the gospel as the Gentiles were. Nevertheless, it does not imply that God’s salvation is no longer being offered to Israel and is only available for the Gentiles. Paul has not completely given up on the Jewish people because he will continue to proclaim the good news of Jesus as Israel’s Messiah to “all” who would come to him (28:30).

Paul’s Bold Proclamation of the Kingdom of God (28:30-31)

The book ends with a summary report of Paul’s imprisonment and testimony in Rome. While in Rome, Paul is allowed to live in his own lodging. It appears that Paul was being held under the most lenient form of military custody. He was able to welcome “all,” whether Jews or Gentiles, who came to him. Acts ends with Paul continuing to proclaim “the kingdom of God” which forms a narrative inclusio (see 1:3,6; 28:23,31) and highlights its proclamation as the central feature of the Christian message.

Conclusion

As historiography, Acts recounts the origins of Christianity, called “the Way” and the expansion of the good news of Jesus Christ starting from Jerusalem to Rome, which for Luke functions as “the ends of the earth” (1:8). Furthermore, Luke wished to defend the peaceful movement of Christianity and the legitimacy of the universal mission of salvation, particularly the Gentile mission. Luke has shown that everything that took place in history happened according to the plan of God. In writing Acts, Luke had a theological goal, which is not just narrating history in general but rather describing a salvation history whereby God’s redemptive activity is being fulfilled “among us” (Luke 1:1). Luke’s somewhat open ending of Acts is intentional, challenging every reader to continue the story and acts of missio Dei (mission of God).
