The Three Schools of Vedanta

All three thinkers accept the same Upanishadic scriptures and the same word for ultimate reality, Brahman. They divide on one question only: what is the relationship between the individual self (atman) and Brahman? Three answers, three schools — learn each by its answer to that one question.

1. Advaita — Non-Dualism (Adi Shankara, c. 800 CE)

Core claim: there is only Brahman; nothing else is ultimately real.

The self and Brahman are not two things that resemble each other — they are identical. What appears to be a separate "you" is Brahman mistaken, under the veil of maya (illusion), for something limited. The scriptural seal: Aham Brahmasmi — "I am Brahman." Path to liberation: jnana (knowledge) alone.

The wave and the ocean: a wave rises on the sea and seems to have its own shape and name, but there is no substance in it other than water. The wave never becomes the ocean and never separates from it, because it was only ever water wearing the appearance of a wave.

A precision worth keeping: Brahman here is finally nirguna, without attributes. A personal God with form belongs to a lower, provisional level of truth.

2. Dvaita — Dualism (Madhvacharya, c. 1238 CE)

Core claim: Brahman (Vishnu) and the individual soul are eternally and completely separate.

The exact opposite of Shankara. Individual souls are dependent (paratantra) on Vishnu, who alone is independent (svatantra). Path to liberation: bhakti (devotion) — and moksha is not becoming God, but entering the eternal loving service of God. The soul keeps its individuality forever, even in liberation.

The sugar-candy: a devotee wishes to taste the sugar-candy, not to become the sugar-candy itself. To taste sweetness, you must remain other than the sweet thing.

Madhva's objection to Shankara: if Ignorance is what binds us, it must belong to something; if it belongs to Brahman, and Brahman is real, then Ignorance is real — which contradicts Advaita's own goal of liberation through removing Ignorance.

3. Vishishtadvaita — Qualified Non-Dualism (Ramanuja, c. 1017–1137 CE)

Core claim: one Brahman, but souls and the world are real parts of Brahman, not illusion.

Ramanuja rejects both extremes. Against Shankara: the world and the self are not illusion. Against Madhva: they are not wholly separate. Brahman here is unambiguously personal (saguna) — a God with real attributes. Path to liberation: bhakti, leading to eternal communion with a personal God, the soul remaining distinct.

The body and its cells: a living body is one organism composed of billions of cells, each with its own identity, none able to exist outside the body. The cells are neither the whole body nor separate from it. So the soul is a real part of Brahman, distinct yet inseparable.

The Comparison, Held Side by Side

The fastest way to hold the whole thing in memory: you are God (Advaita) · you are a part of God (Vishishtadvaita) · you belong to God (Dvaita).

Strip your identity of body and mind — what remains? Advaita says: name it Brahman, for that is all you ever were. Ramanuja says: name it a soul, real and distinct, yet living within God. Madhva says: name it a servant, forever facing the Lord it loves. Indian philosophy does not force one answer; it hands you three lenses and asks which reading of reality you can defend.

The Self and the Absolute in Vedanta and the Christian Confession of God

A dialogical reading of Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita alongside Trinitarian and grace theology — Morning Star Regional Seminary, Barrackpore, BTh Contextual Theology.

The three schools of Vedanta answer a single question in three ways: what is the relation between the individual self and the Absolute. This Essay sets that Vedantic Question beside the Christian Confession of God as Trinity and Giver of Grace — Sankara on non-duality beside the creator–creature distinction and the theology of grace; Ramanuja on the world as the body of God beside the Pauline body of Christ; Madhva on eternal difference and loving dependence beside the bhakti strand of Christian mysticism. The aim is neither concordism nor refutation but a disciplined conversation useful for theology done in India.

The One Question and the Discipline It Demands

A comparison of doctrines is not a search for hidden sameness. The Vedantic schools argue within a Grammar in which liberation (moksha) is release from a beginningless round of birth and death; the Christian confession argues within a Grammar in which God is triune, creation is from nothing, and salvation is the free self-gift of God in Christ. To force one Grammar into the other is to lose both. The honest gain lies elsewhere: each Tradition, pressed by the Questions of the other, is driven back to state its own Claim with more Precision — mutual illumination, not conquest.

Sankara and the Christian Grammar of Creator, Creature and Grace

Between the Creator and the Creature, the Fourth Lateran Council taught, no Likeness can be asserted without a greater Unlikeness always being implied. Read as Rivals, Sankara's Identity-Claim and this Distinction simply contradict each other. Read as mutual Illumination, each sharpens the other: Advaita presses the Christian to confess more strictly that God is not one Being among Beings; the Christian tradition presses Advaita to ask whether the Abolition of all Distinction does justice to Love, which requires an Other to be loved.

This meeting has a concrete Indian History. Brahmabandhab Upadhyay proposed Advaita as the natural philosophical Vestibule for Christian Theology in India; Henri Le Saux (Abhishiktananda) lived the Tension of the advaitic Experience and the Trinitarian Confession without dissolving it — an unresolved Work that is instructive rather than embarrassing.

Ramanuja and the Pauline Body of God

Ramanuja's governing Image, sarira-sariri-bhava, the Relation of Body to embodied Self, meets at once the Pauline Phrase, the Body of Christ (1 Cor 12:12–27; Rom 12:4–5). Both Grammars refuse the same two Errors: collapsing all Difference into an undifferentiated Reality, and scattering Reality into disconnected Units.

The Discipline of the Comparison lies in naming where the Figures diverge. For Ramanuja the Body-Self Relation is metaphysical and permanent, describing the Structure of all Reality. For Paul the Body of Christ is not the Structure of the Cosmos but the new Reality of the Church, gathered by the Spirit through Baptism and the one Bread (1 Cor 10:16–17) — a soteriological and ecclesial Claim, not a Cosmology.

Madhva and the Bhakti Strand of Christian Mysticism

Madhva holds a fivefold and permanent Difference (pancabheda): God alone is svatantra, independent Reality; every Self and every Thing is paratantra, dependent. This is the Vedantic School closest in Structure to Christian bhakti-Mysticism. Teresa of Avila's spiritual Marriage, and Bernard of Clairvaux's Union of Wills — the Drop of Water that seems to lose itself in the Wine yet retains its own Substance — guard against exactly the Reading in which the Creature is simply absorbed.

The Convergence is real; so is the Difference that keeps it honest. For Madhva the eternal Separation is a fixed Law of Being. The Christian bhakta guards the Distinction of Soul and God not because Being decrees an unbridgeable Gulf, but because Love requires two, and because the God who remains Other is the same God who has already crossed the Distance from His own Side in the Incarnation.

Toward a Contextual Reading

Michael Amaladoss reads the advaitin and the bhakta not as Enemies but as two Indian Faces of the one Mystery. George Soares-Prabhu warned that Theology in India suffers a threefold Alienation — from the Indian great Tradition, from the little Traditions of the Poor, and from the liberative Core of the Gospel itself. A Comparison of this kind serves the first Healing only if it does not forget the others: the Self and the Absolute are not discussed in the Abstract by the Labourer of Bengal or the Adivasi of the Forests; the Question of who God is and who I am before God is asked, for them, within Hunger, Displacement and Caste.

Conclusion

Strip the Self of Body and Mind, the Schools say, and name what remains. Advaita answers, name it Brahman, for that is all you ever were. Ramanuja answers, name it a real Part within the one God. Madhva answers, name it a Servant forever facing the Lord it loves. The Christian Confession does not select among these three. It holds that the God who is nearer to me than I am to myself is the same God who stands before me as the Beloved, and that this is possible because God is, in the divine Life itself, a Communion of Persons whose Unity is not the Loss of the Other but its perfect Love.
