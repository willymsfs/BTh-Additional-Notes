### Unit 05, expanded: Vatican II's Copernican turn, and the sense of faith it makes room for

*Lumen Gentium* effects what the literature on this course now calls a Copernican revolution: the Church stops being described primarily as a *societas perfecta*, a hierarchical society complete and self-sufficient in itself, and is described instead, in Chapter 2, as the People of God — a pilgrim people before it is an institution. This is not merely a change of emphasis. It is the reason synodality has any theological ground to stand on at all. LG 10 restores the common priesthood of all the baptised as a genuine, not merely honorific, share in Christ's own priestly office; LG 4 grounds the whole Church in the communion of the Trinity itself, so that her unity is participation in a divine unity, not organisational tidiness. And LG 12 states, in a sentence Catholic teaching has still not finished absorbing, that the whole body of the faithful, from the bishops to the last of the laity, cannot err in matters of belief when it shows universal agreement — the *sensus fidei fidelium*.

Bishop Lawrence Pius, writing on this in the volume behind this update, presses the consequence: within the classical framework the sixteenth-century theologian Melchior Cano gave the discipline, the *sensus fidelium* is a genuine *locus theologicus*, a proper source of theological argument standing alongside Scripture and the councils, not a pastoral courtesy extended to the laity by a hierarchy that could equally withhold it. Augustine had already said something like it centuries earlier: the *concordissima fidei conspiratio*, the most harmonious consensus of the faithful, is itself the voice of the Spirit heard in the whole body. What this dismantles, carefully and not all at once, is a division the post-Tridentine Church had allowed to calcify into something like doctrine itself: the *Ecclesia docens*, the teaching Church, understood as the hierarchy alone, over against the *Ecclesia discens*, the learning Church, understood as everyone else, sitting in passive receipt of what descends from above. Vatican II does not abolish the distinct office of the bishops. It insists that the whole body, not the hierarchy in isolation, is where the Spirit's fidelity to the deposit of faith is actually located and actually heard.

This is also the moment to fix, precisely, how synodality, collegiality and primacy relate — a distinction students reliably blur under exam pressure. Synodality is the wide reality: an attribute of the whole Church as the People of God, in which laity, religious and ordained all share. Collegiality is a specific and narrower expression of it: the particular co-responsibility of the college of bishops, acting *cum et sub Petro*, with and under the Bishop of Rome, for the governance of the universal Church. Primacy — the Pope's own ministry — is not diminished by any of this; it is *re-dimensioned* within it, exercised as what the tradition calls a listening office, the *Unus* who decides only after hearing the *Omnes* and the *Aliqui* (see Unit 08). Three circles, not three competitors: synodality the widest, collegiality inside it, primacy inside that — and every exercise of primacy or collegiality is meant to breathe the wider synodal reality, never to substitute for it.

**Illustration.** Imagine a house with two different blueprints stapled to the same folder, both stamped and authorised by the same architect, neither one ever formally withdrawn. One blueprint draws a pyramid: load-bearing walls running from a single point at the top down through everyone beneath. The other draws a circle of rooms opening onto a shared courtyard, no room load-bearing for any other. Builders working from the first blueprint and builders working from the second are both, honestly, building "what the architect approved" — which is very close to Shalini Mulackal's diagnosis of Vatican II itself: the Council's own texts hold a traditional monarchical-hierarchical ecclesiology and a communio-synodal ecclesiology side by side, unreconciled, so that a bishop insisting on the pyramid and a diocesan council insisting on the courtyard can both, without dishonesty, claim conciliar warrant. This is not evidence the Council failed. It is evidence the building is not yet finished, and that both blueprints are still being worked from in the same Church, sometimes in the same diocese.

**Indian context.** A catechist who has led the same scattered tribal outstations for thirty years frequently knows, with more accuracy than a newly posted diocesan priest, which families are drifting toward a Pentecostal preacher and why, which widow needs a visit before Christmas, and what the Sarhul harvest festival actually means to the baptised families still keeping it. LG 12's claim is not a devotional flourish said about such a person. It is the assertion, meant with full theological seriousness, that her accumulated discernment is itself a *locus theologicus* — and that a diocese which treats her only as a pair of useful hands, never as a source the hierarchy itself has something to learn from, has not yet understood what Vatican II actually taught about who the Church is.

### Unit 06, expanded: the inverted pyramid, and what happened when India tried it

Pope Francis's address at the fiftieth anniversary of the Synod of Bishops, in 2015, supplies the sentence around which this entire course is built: "The path of synodality is the path which God expects from the Church of the third millennium." He does not leave it at exhortation. He gives the claim a structural image — the inverted pyramid — and the image is meant to be taken literally, not decoratively: in the Church, he says, the only genuine height is measured by service, so that those who appear to stand at the summit, the Pope and the bishops, must place themselves underneath everyone else, bearing the weight rather than being borne by it.

The International Theological Commission's 2018 document distinguishes three levels at which this has to be realised, and a synodal Church that gets only one of the three has not yet arrived: the *fundamental style*, the ordinary listening manner in which the Church lives day to day; the *structures and participatory organs* — diocesan synods, pastoral councils, presbyteral councils — through which that style becomes durable rather than a mood; and the *events and assemblies*, the particular synods convoked at a given hour, of which 2021–2024 was the largest example in the Church's history. *Episcopalis Communio* (2018) turns the Synod of Bishops itself from an occasional gathering into a genuine three-phase process — consultation of all the faithful, discernment by the bishops, implementation under the Pope — so that the "listening" is not a preamble to the real decision-making but structurally continuous with it.

What is unusual, and worth teaching in detail, is how visible this became on Indian ground during the 2021–2024 process itself. Bishop Paul Toppo of Raigarh ran diocesan workshops and neighbourhood (*mohalla*) meetings specifically for the laity. Bishop Telesphore Bilung of Jamshedpur made personal parish visits to lay groups, youth and women, and afterward observed that synodality on the ground was functioning as a force to "break the clericalism and ritualism among the priests" — turning the parish pastoral council, in his phrase, from a paper committee into something genuinely collaborative. Archbishop Anil Couto of Delhi formed a special committee to ensure the tribal voice specifically was heard in a metropolitan archdiocese where it is easily drowned out — and noted, in doing so, that tribal Catholic migrants from Chotanagpur, who make up something like seventy per cent of Delhi's Catholic population, had in effect "brought synodality to the Church of Delhi" simply by bringing their own communitarian parish life with them into a city that had largely forgotten it.

The same consultation also recorded its own limits honestly, and a good answer will not omit them: survey data from the process found the poor (33.8% of respondents), the differently-abled (15%) and women (2.3%) still structurally under-represented in the actual consultation — even inside tribal parishes that are, by other measures, unusually participatory (see Unit 12). The inverted pyramid is a genuine theological claim about where the weight belongs. It is not, on this evidence, a claim that has yet finished being built.

**Illustration.** An architect wanting to demonstrate the inverted pyramid does not merely draw it upside down on paper — an upside-down pyramid drawn as a picture is still balanced on its point, still liable to topple onto whatever stands beneath it. The real structural move is different: take the foundation, the widest and heaviest part, and set it down at what used to be the summit, so nothing is left to crush anything underneath, because there is no longer an "underneath" in the old sense at all — only what the foundation now exists to hold up. That is the difference between an image of humility and an actual redistribution of load, and it is the difference Bishop Bilung is describing when he says the parish council in Jamshedpur stopped being a paper committee.

**Indian context.** The Delhi case deserves to be taught as the single sharpest illustration available for this unit, because it reverses the direction everyone expects. The usual assumption is that synodality flows outward from Rome, downward through dioceses, and finally reaches the poor and the migrant last, diluted, if at all. In Delhi it happened the other way: it was the displaced, tribal, economically marginal migrant community that carried a functioning synodal culture — communitarian councils, shared decision-making, the *Mahila Sangh* — into a wealthy metropolitan archdiocese that had largely lost it. The Archbishop did not have to teach the migrants what synodality was. He had to notice that they were already living it, and let it re-teach the diocese.

### Unit 07, expanded: communion — the Trinity as the shape a synodal Church is copying

This is the unit the local course flagged as needing different source material, because its own class-by-class structure had nowhere to put a purely thematic treatment of communion. The theologian who supplies it most directly here is Jacob Parappally, and his argument does something the standard *koinonia*-ecclesiology account (Congar, de Lubac) does not quite do on its own: it grounds synodality not only in how the Church is structured, but in what a human being *is*.

Parappally's claim is that the human person is *imago Trinitatis* — made in the image not of a solitary God but of a communion of Persons — and that this image carries three distinct relational capacities, mirroring Father, Son and Spirit respectively: a capacity for transcendence and radical openness to the other; a capacity for transparency, vulnerability, and the willingness to share what one holds, including authority; and a capacity for interiority and discernment. Read this way, community breakdown is never a mysterious or merely circumstantial failure. It is always traceable to one of these three capacities going unused — a refusal of the other, a refusal to be transparent or to share what is held, or a refusal of the patient interior attentiveness discernment requires. The Trinity itself, in this reading, is characterised by *perichoresis*, the mutual indwelling of the three Persons in an eternal exchange of equals with no hierarchy of domination whatsoever — and the synodal Church is being asked to mirror that exchange on earth, which is a considerably harder demand than being asked merely to hold more meetings.

Bishop Lawrence Pius supplies the sacramental anchor for the same claim. Following the ITC, he insists the Church does not merely *hold* synods from time to time; she *is* a synod — her *modus vivendi et operandi*, her very way of living and operating. And the place where this becomes physically, weekly true is the Eucharist: the primary synodal event, where distinctions of status, class and gender are — at least in principle, at the moment of reception — relativised before the table of the Lord. This is why the tradition has always treated communion ecclesiology and Eucharistic theology as one enquiry rather than two: a Church that has structurally separated who may approach the altar, and how, has not merely committed a pastoral lapse. She has contradicted, at her own centre, the very reality the rest of her synodal structures exist to build.

**Illustration.** Picture an ordinary Sunday Mass in a mixed parish: a landowner and the labourer who works his fields, a doctor and the woman who cleans the presbytery, kneel at the same altar rail within a few minutes of each other and receive from the same priest's hand, the same host, without distinction. For that half-minute, the social order that governs every other hour of their week is simply not operative. Nobody has legislated it away; the sacrament has made it, briefly and really, untrue. Every synodal structure this course studies — councils, consultations, the *Omnes-Aliqui-Unus* schema of Unit 08 — is best understood as the Church's attempt to extend that half-minute of the Eucharist into the other hundred and sixty-seven hours of the week.

**Indian context.** This is precisely why caste discrimination at the communion rail is not treated in the theological literature as merely a grave pastoral failure alongside other grave pastoral failures, but as a distinct category of wound. Where local custom has separated communion queues or seating by caste — a documented, not hypothetical, reality in parts of India — the volume behind this update, drawing on George Soares-Prabhu's reading of Jesus's own table fellowship, does not hesitate to call it a parody of the Eucharist: the joyous, boundary-abolishing meal of Jesus turned back into a caste meal. A synodal Church cannot claim the Eucharist as her constitutive act of communion in one breath and tolerate a divided table in the next; the two claims cancel each other, and it is the second one, in practice, that usually wins unless it is named and corrected.

### Unit 08, expanded: participation, and the three subjects who make a decision genuinely synodal

Cyprian's threefold rule from Unit 04 — nothing without the bishop, nothing without the council of presbyters, nothing without the consent of the people — receives its fullest modern working-out in a schema this course's sources call *Omnes, Aliqui, Unus*: all, some, one. It deserves to be learned as a single diagram, because it is the tool that prevents synodality collapsing into either of its two false alternatives.

The **Omnes** — all — are the whole body, consulted without exclusion: the *sensus fidei* of every baptised person genuinely listened to, which Unit 05 has already grounded theologically. The **Aliqui** — some — are the representative bodies: councils, chapters, synodal assemblies, whose task is not simply to relay what was heard but to *discern* it, synthesising raw consultation into an actual proposal. The **Unus** — one — is the office bearer holding the ministry of unity: parish priest, bishop, or Pope, according to level, whose task is to *decide* — but critically, only in, with, and after hearing both the Omnes and the Aliqui. Remove the Unus from this picture and what remains is a parliament, deciding truth by majority, which Pope Francis has explicitly and repeatedly said a synod is not. Remove the Omnes and the Aliqui, and what remains is an unaccountable monarchy wearing synodal vocabulary. The schema's entire discipline lies in refusing either simplification.

This is also where the gap between theology and canon law becomes impossible to hide. Merlin Rengith Ambrose, drawing on the German canonist Sabine Demel, names it precisely: because canon law defines diocesan and parish councils as consultative only, lay co-responsibility is reduced in practice to a *concession* granted by clerical authority, revocable at will, rather than a *right* rooted in baptism itself (c. 208). Demel and Ambrose's proposed correction reaches for a change of standing, not merely of function: laity formed and treated as **co-believers**, sharing ownership of the Church's mission by baptismal right, rather than as **collaborators**, assisting a mission that remains someone else's. It is a small change of noun with a large change of theology behind it.

**Illustration.** Think of how a substantial Indian family wedding actually gets decided, as distinct from how the invitation card presents it. The whole extended village or *mohalla* is fed and welcomed — that is the *Omnes*, genuinely included, genuinely part of the occasion. A tighter circle of close family sits together over several evenings actually working out dates, budget, and who sits where — that is the *Aliqui*, discerning and shaping the concrete plan from what the wider circle's hopes and constraints have made clear. And the elders of both families, usually the fathers, still give the decisive word that fixes the date and seals the arrangement — the *Unus*. No one at such a wedding mistakes the elders' final word for a decision made in isolation from the other two circles; and no one mistakes the wide, genuine welcome given to the whole village for the same thing as that final, binding word. The Church's schema asks for exactly this discipline, at every level from parish to Rome.

**Indian context.** Two figures worth holding together. Susairaj's finding that Indian dioceses spend roughly eighty per cent of total financial resources on the formation and upkeep of priests and religious — some five per cent of the Catholic population — while the remaining ninety-five per cent receive almost no theological, canonical or leadership formation, shows that participation is starved structurally, by a budget line, before it is ever refused attitudinally. And yet, in tribal parishes specifically, the *Mahila Sangh*, the women's council, already functions in 74.4% of parishes — more widespread even than the parish council itself at 69.8% — proof that where formation and custom have allowed it, the *Aliqui* forms itself readily, without waiting for permission from the budget that is supposed to be funding it.

### Unit 10, expanded: Conversation in the Spirit — the method behind the listening

The Synod's working method has a name, Conversation in the Spirit, and a biblical template: the Council of Jerusalem already studied in Unit 03. What this unit adds is the theological reasoning behind *why* the method takes the specific shape it does, since students who can recite the three steps without understanding the reasoning tend to apply the method as an empty procedure rather than as genuine discernment.

Jude Nirmal Doss supplies the pneumatological foundation, following Yves Congar's insistence that a sound pneumatological ecclesiology must be balanced by a pneumatological Christology: the same Spirit who anointed Christ at his own baptism now animates the whole Body, not merely its office-holders. Crucially, Doss stresses that the Spirit is the source of *plurality* as well as unity — producing harmony out of genuinely diverse charisms, not demanding uniformity — which functions as the method's built-in guard against groupthink. A "consensus" reached by quietly suppressing disagreement has not undergone Conversation in the Spirit; it has merely avoided it.

John Alexander works out the epistemology this implies: in synodal discernment, authority (*potestas*) is subordinately bound to discernment (*discretio*) — the bishop or superior does not create truth in isolation and then announce it, but gathers, synthesises, and judicially confirms a discernment the community has already reached together. He also insists on the correct sequence of the method's two postures: *parrhesia* (Acts 4:31), courageous and transparent speech, must come *before* the silence, because silence without something true having first been risked aloud is not discernment — it is merely the absence of conflict, with nothing yet to sit with. Lawrence Culas locates the actual safeguard with equal precision in the *placement* of Acts 15:12's "the entire assembly kept silence," coming immediately after the apostles have spoken: not a general request to listen well, but a structural, mandated pause, which is what actually prevents a conversation sliding into rebuttal and counter-rebuttal — very different from asking people to be attentive while everyone is still talking.

Culas also supplies the standing caution that keeps this from becoming naive: *vox populi* is not automatically *vox Dei*. Parish and diocesan elections in India, he notes candidly, are frequently marred by groupism and ethnic lobbying — the ordinary mechanics of faction politics, indistinguishable in outward form from genuine discernment unless something more than vote-counting is actually present. The method's spiritual demands — courageous speech, mandated silence, discernment prior to decision — are not decoration on top of an otherwise procedural consensus process. They are the only thing that distinguishes it from one.

**Illustration.** A courtroom offers the clearest secular analogue. Witnesses testify — that is the *parrhesia*, courageous speech risked in public, under cross-examination, where softening the truth carries real cost. Then the jury is sequestered, alone, in a room with no further testimony permitted — and that silence is not empty waiting; it is the room in which everything just heard is actually weighed rather than merely registered. Only after that mandated pause does the jury return a verdict — and a jury that skipped the sequestration, announcing its decision the moment the last witness sat down, would rightly be regarded as having failed to deliberate at all, however confident its verdict sounded.

**Indian context.** Culas's warning about groupism in parish and diocesan elections is not an abstract caution imported from political science; it names something students preparing for parish ministry in Bengal will recognise directly from council elections they have already witnessed — factions forming along language, ethnicity, or old family rivalry well before any spiritual discernment gets underway. The remedy the method proposes is not to abolish elections, but to insist that *parrhesia* and mandated silence happen inside the process, not around it — which is precisely the discipline most parish councils, under time pressure, are tempted to skip.

### Unit 11, expanded: authority re-examined — collegiality, primacy, and the canonical bottleneck

Cyprian's threefold rule from Unit 04 — bishop, presbyterate, people, none dispensable — turns out to be strikingly close to the *Omnes-Aliqui-Unus* schema of Unit 08, separated by some seventeen centuries. What has changed since Cyprian's day, and what this unit exists to examine, is that the third element of his rule — the consent of the people — has in the Latin Church's own canon law been formalised into something considerably weaker than consent: consultation.

The mechanism is precise and worth learning by canon number. Canon 129 §1 restricts the *potestas regiminis*, the power of governance, to those in sacred orders, permitting the laity only to "co-operate" in its exercise. Parish Pastoral Councils (c. 536), Parish Finance Councils (c. 537), and Diocesan Pastoral Councils (c. 511) are, as a direct consequence, defined as consultative rather than deliberative bodies. The practical effect, as Bishop S. Antonysamy sets out plainly: a council may reach unanimous consensus on a matter, and the bishop or parish priest may legally decide the opposite, without violating a single canon. This is the mechanism behind what Unit 08 already named as the reduction of lay co-responsibility to a revocable concession rather than a baptismal right.

Two comparisons, one internal to Catholicism and one external, show this is a contingent legislative choice rather than a theological necessity. Internally: the Eastern Catholic Churches governed by the CCEO operate quite differently. Varghese Koluthara notes that, unlike the Latin Synod of Bishops, which remains purely advisory, the Synods of the Patriarchal and Major Archiepiscopal Churches — including India's own Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara Churches — hold genuine legislative, executive and judicial power: electing their own heads, erecting eparchies, enacting particular law without needing prior Roman approval. The Latin Church's synodal reform, on this reading, is not requesting something without Catholic precedent; it is requesting to catch up with a canonical model that already operates inside the same communion. Externally, Simon Pinto points to the Anglican Communion's three-house system — Bishops, Clergy, and Laity — in which major doctrinal, moral and financial decisions require the *concurrent deliberative* approval of all three houses, a live demonstration within a tradition that also claims apostolic ministry that a binding lay vote does not, in fact, dissolve that claim.

**Illustration.** The difference between a consultative and a deliberative structure is exactly the difference between an advisory committee and a shareholding board. A company's advisory committee may study a proposal for months, produce a unanimous, well-reasoned recommendation — and the chief executive remains legally free to file it and do the opposite, because an advisory opinion, however excellent, was never a binding vote. A board with actual voting shares cannot be so easily overruled, because its "yes" or "no" is constitutive of the decision, not merely input into it. Canon law currently gives the Catholic laity, structurally, the advisory committee. The theological argument of this course — from Cyprian's *consensus plebis* onward — is that baptism was always meant to grant something closer to the shareholding board.

**Indian context.** Indian seminarians preparing for ministry in a country with two living Eastern Catholic traditions of their own do not need to travel to Constantinople for a working alternative to consultative-only structures; the Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara Synods, exercising real legislative and judicial authority under the CCEO, are a domestic, Indian instance of exactly the deliberative model the Latin synodal reform is still only requesting for itself.

### Unit 12, expanded: the Indian context in full — tribal governance, caste, and the honest limits of the consultation

This unit carries the largest weight of any in the course, and deserves it: the source behind this update is, in its entirety, a study of synodality specifically in the Indian and tribal Church, and its material belongs almost wholly here.

**Tribal governance as pre-existing synods.** Long before the word "synodality" reached India, tribal and regional governance structures were already reaching decisions by consensus rather than by counting votes — independently arriving at something close to the method of Acts 15, for the identical underlying reason: a majority vote leaves a defeated minority still living inside the same small community the following morning, which the community cannot afford. The Khasi *Durbar* operates in two tiers, the *Durbar Kur* (clan council, led by the *Rangbahkur*) and the *Durbar Shnong* (village council, led by the *Rangbah-shnong*). Among the Tangkhul Naga, the village headman, the *Awunga*, is restrained by a council of councillors, the *Hangva*, under customary law that leaves — in the sources' own phrase — "no room for the Awunga to be despotic." Kuki chieftainship, though nominally monarchic, cannot act unilaterally, being bound by customary law enforced through a participatory village council. And the Munda, Ho, Santhal and Oraon village assemblies reach their decisions through patient dialogue continued until consensus forms, never through a show of hands.

**Tribal parish life today.** The same instinct carries into parish structures: the *Mahila Sangh* functions in 74.4% of tribal parishes, more widely than the parish council itself (69.8%); the *Yuva Sangh* in 67.4%; catechists function, in effect, as the *Aliqui* of the tribal Church, leading Sunday worship across dozens of scattered outstations no priest can regularly reach.

**The 2021–2024 consultation on tribal ground.** As Unit 06 has already detailed, Bishops Toppo and Bilung and Archbishop Couto each ran the global consultation through genuinely local method — workshops, personal visits, a dedicated committee for the tribal voice — with Bilung specifically crediting the process with beginning to "break the clericalism and ritualism among the priests." And as that unit also recorded honestly, the same process's own survey data found the poor, the differently-abled and women still under-represented even here (33.8%, 15%, 2.3% respectively) — evidence that a genuinely participatory culture and a genuinely complete consultation are not automatically the same achievement.

**Caste, and the critique of synodality itself.** Against this, the volume does not permit a triumphalist reading. Upper-caste dominance persists in clerical appointments and in the seminary; casteism is named as one of the most damaging Indian variants of clericalism specifically; and the sources record the uncomfortable finding that Dalit clergy, once ordained, not infrequently adopt the dominant clerical mindset themselves rather than remaining in solidarity with the community they came from. James Ponniah's distinction between *niti*, the formal institutional declaration of synodality, and *nyaya*, the lived experience of justice on the ground, names precisely why a diocese can accumulate an admirable record of the first while the second stays untouched. Shalini Mulackal's diagnosis, already met in Unit 05, explains why this is not merely local failure of will: Vatican II's own unreconciled dual ecclesiology gives cover to both the councils that meet faithfully and the centralisation that continues to override them. And the 2021–2024 process specifically has been criticised for being reached, on the ground, chiefly by an educated, English-speaking, urban parish elite — with poor Dalit Christians and illiterate Adivasi Catholics largely bypassed by online questionnaires that assumed a literacy and connectivity the consultation's own intended beneficiaries frequently do not have.

**Indigenous precedent, before Vatican II had the word.** It is worth closing this unit on a genuinely hopeful note that is not naive: before the Synod of Diamper (1599) imposed Latin structures on the ancient St Thomas Christian community, that community was governed by a three-tier lay-clerical assembly — the *Palliyogam*, *Desiyayogam* and *Mahayogam* — with a native lay Archdeacon, the *Jathikkukarthavian*, holding genuine executive power. India did not need synodality imported. It had already built, and then had taken from it, a working version of its own.

**Illustration.** A Khasi village facing a genuinely difficult dispute does not settle it by a show of hands at the *Durbar Shnong*. Every party with a stake speaks, at length, sometimes returning to points already made; the *Rangbah-shnong* does not interrupt to move things along; and the meeting simply continues, without a scheduled end time, until the room's mood visibly settles into agreement — at which point the decision is recorded as having been reached, not decreed. A Western observer accustomed to motions, seconds and votes might mistake the whole proceeding for a failure to decide anything. It is, in fact, a working model of exactly the consensus discernment Acts 15 describes, running on custom rather than borrowed vocabulary.

**Indian context.** Hold the two halves of this unit together rather than choosing between them, because the sources themselves refuse to let either stand alone: the tribal Church is, in real and specific ways, already living what the rest of the Catholic world is still trying to learn to do — and the same Indian Church has allowed caste to survive inside her own structures with a durability that should trouble anyone tempted to treat synodality as a problem solved wherever tribal custom happens to prevail. Both are true on the same page of the same source, and a synodal theology honest enough to teach both is worth more than one that teaches only the flattering half.

### Unit 13, expanded: ecumenism — where Catholic practice still has ground to make up

Synodality's ecumenical dimension is not a footnote appended to internal Catholic reform; several of the traditions the Church is in dialogue with have already institutionalised structures Catholic canon law is only beginning to request for itself, which makes this unit a genuine two-way conversation rather than a one-directional Catholic export.

The Orthodox tradition offers *sobornost* — a Russian theological term meaning something like catholicity, conciliarity, or spiritual harmony — in which synodality and collegiality are not separate tracks at all: the local eucharistic community under its bishop is understood as naturally, constitutively synodal, and no bishop is considered to act legitimately outside the college of bishops or apart from the consent of his own flock. Varghese Koluthara notes that in the Catholic Church's own dialogue with Orthodoxy, this has already been recognised as a resource for rebalancing the historically strained relationship between papal primacy and episcopal collegiality — the West, in effect, relearning from the East a synodal instinct it once shared more fully.

The Anglican Communion, together with various Reformed bodies, offers something structurally sharper still: a three-house governing system — Bishops, Clergy, and Laity — in which major doctrinal, moral and financial decisions require the *concurrent deliberative* approval of all three houses, not merely the consultation of two of them by the third. As already noted in Unit 11, Simon Pinto reads this as a direct and practical challenge to Catholic canonists: it demonstrates, within a tradition that also claims apostolic ministry, that granting the laity a genuinely deliberative — not merely consultative — vote does not, in fact, destroy that claim. The objection Catholic canon law implicitly makes to a binding lay vote is therefore shown to be a matter of institutional choice, defensible or not on its own terms, rather than an unavoidable theological necessity forced by the nature of apostolic order itself.

**Illustration.** Picture two different legislatures. In the first, a bill becomes law only once it has passed three separate houses, each with genuine power to block it, forcing real negotiation among all three before anything is final. In the second, two houses may debate a bill at length and vote on it unanimously, but the law only takes effect once a single upper chamber, unbound by either vote, chooses to ratify it — and may, entirely within its own rules, simply decline. The Anglican three-house model is the first kind of legislature. The Catholic Church's current canon law, for all its ITC documents and Conversation in the Spirit, is still functioning, structurally, as something much closer to the second.

**Indian context.** Bengal's own Christian landscape makes this unit unusually concrete rather than merely comparative: Catholic seminarians here train for ministry alongside, and in regular contact with, Protestant and Orthodox Christian communities whose own governance already embodies some of what this course is asking the Catholic Church to grow into. And — a point worth making explicitly, since students rarely connect the two — India's Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara Synods, examined in Unit 11 as an internal Catholic comparison, function almost as a *domestic* ecumenical bridge as well: a Catholic seminarian in Bengal need not travel to an Orthodox or Anglican context to encounter a working model closer to *sobornost* than to Latin curial practice. It already exists inside the Indian Catholic communion itself.
