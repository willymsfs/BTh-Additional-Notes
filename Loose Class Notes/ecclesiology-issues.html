I.

Communion, Primacy, and Structure

How the one Church relates to its many local churches, and who exercises authority over the whole — from Rome, from the ancient patriarchates, and from the bishops gathered regionally.

01

The Universal Church and the Local Church: Which Is Prior?

Every account of the Church's basic shape must decide whether the one universal Church precedes and grounds the many local churches, or whether the two co-arise together. The question resurfaced sharply in the Ratzinger–Kasper exchange of the early 2000s and remains active in Catholic–Orthodox eucharistic ecclesiology.

Universal Priority

Joseph Ratzinger, the CDF's 1992 letter Communionis Notio, Guy Mansini, and Avery Dulles hold that the Church pre-exists in the mind of God and was given at Pentecost as one reality that then generates the local churches; the Petrine office belongs to the interior constitution of every local church, not merely to relations between them.

Simultaneity

Walter Kasper and Richard Gaillardetz, joined from the Orthodox side by John Zizioulas and Nicholas Afanasiev, argue the universal and local are equiprimordial, realized together at Pentecost in the concrete assembly at Jerusalem. Afanasiev goes furthest: "where the Eucharist is, there is the Church," in full, with no room for a jurisdictional universal above it.

MTh AngleTrace the argument from Communionis Notio (1992) through Kasper's 2001 reply into its reception in current Catholic–Orthodox dialogue, and test whether "equiprimordiality" is a genuine third option or a restatement of one side in softer language.

In this notebookT&T Clark Handbook of Ecclesiology; Ecclesiology (Mansini); Ecclesiology for a Global Church (Gaillardetz); Ecclesiology at the Beginning of the Third Millennium.

02

Petrine Primacy: Juridical Supremacy or Eucharistic Presidency?

Pastor Aeternus (1870) defined papal jurisdiction as full, supreme, ordinary, and immediate. Whether that definition can be reconciled with patristic conciliarism or modern ecumenism is unfinished business, and John Paul II said as much in 1995.

Juridical Primacy

Vatican I and Guy Mansini tie immediate papal jurisdiction to the ontological priority of the universal Church (Debate 01). Miroslav Volf argues, critically, that Ratzinger's trinitarian starting point structurally requires a monarchical papacy.

Synodal Primacy

Paul McPartlan and Henri de Lubac anchor the papacy in the local church of Rome via Cyprian's "the episcopate is one." John Zizioulas holds primacy necessary at every level but only exercisable synodically; the Moscow Patriarchate's 2012 paper accepts no more than a non-jurisdictional "primacy of honour."

MTh AngleUt Unum Sint's invitation to "find a new way of exercising primacy... open to a new situation" is still unanswered magisterially — a live constructive-ecumenism topic rather than a historical one.

In this notebookSacrament of Salvation (McPartlan); Dumitru Staniloae's Trinitarian Ecclesiology (Coman); Ecclesiology (Mansini).

03

Episcopal Conferences: Doctrinal Authority or Consultative Body?

Vatican II created episcopal conferences without settling their doctrinal status — a gap Francis has reopened and that touches every question of inculturation and local pastoral practice.

Rome-Dependent

John Paul II's Apostolos Suos (1998) restricts binding conference statements to unanimous votes or Roman recognitio. Ratzinger called conferences theologically baseless, producing "flattened," lowest-common-denominator documents; de Lubac distinguishes a mere "collective act" from a true "collegial act."

Sound Decentralization

Francis's Evangelii Gaudium laments that conferences' "genuine doctrinal authority" was never elaborated and calls for decentralization. Gaillardetz calls Apostolos Suos "theologically inadequate"; Tillard grounds conferences in eucharistic collegialis affectus. Mariusz Biliniewicz warns this risks real doctrinal pluralism — divergent conference rulings on, e.g., communion for the divorced and remarried.

MTh AngleA case study — the German Synodal Way, or the Amazon Synod's regional proposals — tests whether "sound decentralization" is operative doctrine or aspiration.

In this notebookEcclesiology for a Global Church (Gaillardetz); T&T Clark Handbook of Ecclesiology.

04

East and West: Filioque, Pentarchy, and the Limits of Analogy

Behind the primacy debate lies a deeper trinitarian disagreement about how "the one" and "the many" relate — expressed liturgically in the filioque clause and structurally in the ancient pentarchy of patriarchates.

Orthodox / Pentarchic

Dumitru Stăniloae argues the filioque splits Christology from pneumatology, producing an "essence-centred," over-institutionalized Western ecclesiology that hinders "personal freedom and diversity"; Lossky and Romanides trace most other Western deviations to this root. The pentarchy of five sees, not a Roman monarchy, is read as the Church's proper conciliar structure.

Roman / Universal

Pastor Aeternus and Guy Mansini defend an ordinary, immediate universal jurisdiction that does not require synodal consensus. Zizioulas, writing from within Orthodoxy but in ecumenical dialogue, allows a qualified universal primacy bound to synodality — a mediating position both sides contest.

MTh AngleThe 2007 Ravenna Document and the Moscow Patriarchate's dissenting response are a compact, self-contained primary-source pair for a chapter on whether "sobornic liberty" and Roman primacy can in fact be reconciled.

In this notebookDumitru Staniloae's Trinitarian Ecclesiology (Coman); The Ecclesiology of St. Nicephorus I (O'Connell); The Ecclesiology of St. Basil the Great (Druzhinina).

II.

Authority and Boundaries

What the Magisterium can bind, who else counts as Church, and how far "listening" can go before it dissolves the teaching office it claims to renew.

05

Infallibility: Küng's Challenge and the Rahner/von Balthasar Response

Hans Küng's Infallible? An Inquiry (1971) used Humanae Vitae to argue that even solemnly proposed teaching can err — a direct challenge to Vatican I that provoked two very different rebuttals from within the Catholic academy.

Küng: Indefectibility, Not Infallibility

Dogmatic language is historically conditioned and can be wrong; church authority rests not on an infallible organ but on the Spirit keeping the pilgrim community indefectible in truth despite individual, even magisterial, error.

Rahner & von Balthasar: Defended, Reframed

Rahner rejects Küng's use of Humanae Vitae as a test case — he denies it was ever proposed as infallible — while defending infallibility's legitimacy as a secondary, conflict-resolving mechanism rather than the primary act of faith. Von Balthasar's Der antirömische Affekt (1974) is a sharper polemical defense, framing the era's attacks on the papacy as a "peasant rebellion" against hierarchy.

MTh AngleBecause Rahner and Küng agree that infallible statements are still historically conditioned in their expression, the live question is narrower than the public dispute suggested — precisely where Rahner draws a line Küng erases.

In this notebookA Critical Study of Hans Küng's Ecclesiology (Simuț); The Ecclesiology of Karl Rahner (Lennan); An Introduction to Ecclesiology (Kärkkäinen).

06

"Subsistit In": Who Else Counts as Church?

Lumen Gentium 8 replaced "is" (est) with "subsists in" (subsistit in) to describe the relation of Christ's Church to the Catholic Church — a single verb bearing enormous ecumenical weight ever since.

Exclusive Reading

Sebastian Tromp, who drafted the term, and Ratzinger/the CDF's Dominus Iesus (2000) read subsistit as technical and exclusive: the Church of Christ as a "concrete agent" exists only in the Catholic Church; other bodies are "ecclesial communities," not Churches, lacking valid episcopate and Eucharist.

Non-Exclusive Reading

Edward Schillebeeckx reads subsistit as meaning "not exactly identical to"; Francis Sullivan argues for "non-exclusive identity," allowing that other communities can in some respects realize communion with Christ more fully than some Catholics do; Kasper reads the term as an admission of the Catholic Church's own imperfection and sin.

MTh AngleSullivan's 2006–08 Theological Studies exchange with Karl Becker is a self-contained, citation-rich dispute — ideal for a close-reading seminar paper rather than a broad survey.

In this notebookEcclesiology (Mansini); T&T Clark Handbook of Ecclesiology; A Church with Open Doors (Gaillardetz).

07

Synodality: Listening Church or Doctrinal Pluralism?

Francis's synodal reforms revive the collegiality question (Debate 03) at the level of the whole Church's process, not only its regional structures.

The Listening Church

Francis's vision, grounded in a baptismal sensus fidelium, and Gaillardetz's "pastoral magisterium" of doctrinal humility, treat controverted questions as properly open to ongoing discernment rather than pre-emptively closed.

The Bounded Magisterium

Biliniewicz's critique of a "hearsay magisterium" and Cardinal Müller's warning that a pastorally-positivist magisterium leaves the Church "stranded on a sandbank"; von Balthasar had already warned that decentralization could produce a bureaucratic "photocopying church."

MTh AngleThe Synod on Synodality's own 2021–24 documents are recent enough that a chapter here is original engagement with primary sources, not literature review.

In this notebookEcclesiology for a Global Church (Gaillardetz); The Turn to the Church in the Twentieth and Twenty-First Centuries (Mulder).

III.

Church, Body, and the Marginalized

Two places where ecclesiology meets bodies directly: whose body can stand at the altar in persona Christi, and whose body — the materially poor — the Church claims as its own flesh.

08

Women's Ordination and Sacramental Representation

Inter Insigniores (1976) and Ordinatio Sacerdotalis (1994) ground an all-male priesthood in the claim that the priest, acting in persona Christi, needs a "natural resemblance" to the male Christ.

Ontological Complementarity

The magisterial line — Paul VI, John Paul II's Mulieris Dignitatem, Ratzinger's 2004 CDF letter — treats sexual difference as ontological and complementary; only a male can iconically represent Christ the Bridegroom at the altar.

Baptismal Reconstruction

Elizabeth Johnson uses Gregory of Nazianzus's axiom — "what is not assumed is not saved" — to argue that if maleness is essential to representing Christ, women are excluded from redemption itself. Mary Ann Hinsdale calls complementarity "the issue under the issues"; Rosemary Radford Ruether's separatist "women-church" and Teresa Berger and Sarah Coakley's readings of gender fluidity already present in the tradition (a nursing, labouring eucharistic body) offer different routes past the same wall.

MTh AngleJohnson's patristic-axiom argument is a compact, well-defined thesis a dissertation could test against the fuller range of patristic uses of the axiom, beyond its narrow citation in the ordination debate.

In this notebookA Church with Open Doors (Gaillardetz); T&T Clark Handbook of Ecclesiology.

09

Liberation Ecclesiology: Church of the Poor or Politicized Church?

Medellín (1968) and Puebla (1979) put "the poor" at the centre of Latin American ecclesiology, provoking a Roman response that still shapes how "base" and "hierarchy" are understood to relate.

Church of the Poor

Gutiérrez redefines theology as critical reflection on praxis with the poor as its "interlocutor"; Boff's "ecclesiogenesis" reads Base Ecclesial Communities as a new, lay-and-word-centred way of being Church, not a mere sociological response to clergy shortage. Sobrino's extra pauperes nulla salus names the poor as the Church's sacrament of Christ; Romero embodied the same claim pastorally.

The Institutional Response

Ratzinger and the CDF's Libertatis Nuntius criticize the Marxist social analysis behind this move as an "immanentist" politicization that strips the People of God of its sacramental character, insisting the primary liberation is from sin — and warning that bypassing hierarchical oversight for autonomous base communities risks new, unchecked abuses of power.

MTh AngleBoff's own claim that base communities are "not distinct from the institutional Church" sits in real tension with Ratzinger's fear of an alternative "Church from below." Locating exactly where that tension lives would make a tight, defensible thesis chapter.

In this notebookA Liberation Ecclesiology (Corkery); An Introduction to Ecclesiology (Kärkkäinen); T&T Clark Handbook of Ecclesiology.
